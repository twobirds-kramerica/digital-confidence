Module 2: Your Security Shield

What you will learn: How to spot the tricks scammers use — and what to do when a call, email, or message feels wrong.

Dorothy's Story

Dorothy, 71, was at home one Thursday afternoon when her phone rang. The caller said he was from the Canada Revenue Agency. He knew her name. He said she owed $1,400 in back taxes and would be arrested within the hour if she did not pay immediately.

Dorothy was terrified. She asked if she could call back. "No," the caller said. "You must pay now. Get gift cards from a pharmacy and read us the numbers over the phone."

Dorothy hung up. She called her daughter. It was a scam — one of the most common ones in Canada.

Tell me more about how the caller knew her name

Names are surprisingly easy to find online — phone directories, old social media posts, local news coverage. Knowing your name does not mean a caller is legitimate.

The three signs that gave the scam away: the threat of immediate arrest, the urgency that would not allow a callback, and the demand for gift cards. Real government agencies do not work this way. We will look at exactly what they do and do not do later in this lesson.

The 3-Second Rule

When a call, a text, or an email creates urgency — stop for three seconds.

Scammers rely on panic. They want you to act before you think. The moment someone tells you to act immediately, that is your signal to slow down.

In those three seconds, ask yourself: Did I start this conversation? A real bank, a real government agency, a real company — none of them mind if you hang up and call back on a number you find yourself.

Tell me more — what do I do after those three seconds?

The most powerful move: hang up. Then call the organisation back using a phone number from their official website or from the back of your bank card. Scammers cannot intercept that call.

If it was a real call, the organisation will understand. If it was a scam, you just protected yourself. Either way, nothing bad happens from calling back.

Fake Emails and Texts

A "phishing" message is a fake email or text designed to look real. It might look like it came from your bank, Canada Post, or the government.

These messages often say your account is in trouble, or that you have a package waiting. They ask you to tap a link.

The one rule: never tap a link in a message that surprised you. If you think the message might be real, close it, open your browser, and go to the organisation's website yourself by typing the address.

Tell me more about how to spot a fake

Real phishing messages often look almost perfect. They use the right logos and colours. The giveaway is almost always the urgency and the request.

Your bank will never ask you to "confirm your password" by clicking a link in an email. Canada Post does not require payment to release a package via text message. When in doubt, go directly to the source — never through a link someone sent you.

What the CRA Will Never Do

Knowing what a real government agency will never do makes it easy to spot a fake one.

The Canada Revenue Agency will never: threaten you with immediate arrest, ask you to pay with gift cards, demand payment over the phone without sending a letter first, or refuse to let you call back on a number you found yourself.

If someone on the phone says they are from the government and does any of these things — it is a scam. Full stop.

Tell me more — where do I report a scam?

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre receives tens of thousands of reports every year. You do not need to be a victim to report — suspicion is enough.

Contact them at 1-888-495-8501 or at antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca. Reporting helps protect others in your community.

Your Security Shield Is Up

Here is what you learned in this lesson:

  • The 3-Second Rule: when pressure is high, slow down. You can always call back on a number you find yourself.
  • Scammers use urgency and fear. A real organisation will never mind if you hang up and call back.
  • Never tap links in messages that surprised you. Go to the website yourself by typing the address.
  • The CRA will never demand gift card payment or threaten immediate arrest. If they do, it is a scam.

One thing you can do today: Find the phone number on the back of your bank card. Save it in your contacts as "My Bank — Official Number." That way, if you ever get a suspicious call claiming to be your bank, you know exactly where to call back.
