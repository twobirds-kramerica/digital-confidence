## Q1. What kids actually do to break digital rules, by age band

Caveat up front: no single study maps "digital rule-breaking" cleanly onto grades 5-12 with a clean most-common/highest-harm ranking — this section synthesizes across several sources rather than citing one authoritative table. Treat the age bands as directional, not precise.

**Grades 5-6 (~10-12yo):** Mostly boundary-testing on *access*, not deception-heavy yet — using a device/app past the allowed time, downloading a game or app that wasn't approved, watching age-inappropriate video content, using a sibling's or friend's login to get around an app-store age gate. Common Sense Media's longitudinal data shows this age already has meaningfully high device access (tablets by age 2 in many households) which sets up early habituation to unsupervised use ([Common Sense Census 2025](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight)).

**Grades 7-8 (~12-14yo):** This is where active *deception* starts appearing as a named behaviour in the research: secondary/"finsta" accounts kept hidden from parents (68% of teen Instagram users maintain a finsta per Pew-cited 2023 data), lying about who they're talking to, deleting browsing/message history, using a friend's device to access blocked platforms. Clearfork Academy and similar clinical-adjacent sources describe this age band using shared-password workarounds and "loophole" apps (calculator vaults, second messaging apps) specifically to defeat parental controls ([How Teens Keep Online Activities Hidden from Parents](https://clearforkacademy.com/blog/how-teens-keep-online-activities-hidden-from-parents/); [Pew: Parents, Teens, and Online Privacy](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2012/11/20/parents-teens-and-online-privacy/)).

**Grades 9-10 (~14-16yo):** VPN use to bypass network-level or school-level filtering becomes common and is explicitly documented as a workaround method, not just a privacy tool ([Clearfork Academy](https://clearforkacademy.com/blog/how-teens-keep-online-activities-hidden-from-parents/)). This is also the age band with the highest legal exposure: sexting and non-consensual image sharing peak here, which is why 13+ US states run formal court-diversion programs specifically for this age group ([Cyberbullying Research Center — sexting laws](https://cyberbullying.org/the-status-of-sexting-laws-across-the-united-states)). A prospective JMIR study found that strict parental internet rules still *reduce* problematic social media use at this age, but the protective effect weakens as adolescents approach 15.7 years and can even reverse (rules predicting *more* problematic use in older teens) ([JMIR 2025, Parental Internet-Specific Rules](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e64252/PDF); [PMC summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12445780/)) — i.e., a program aimed at 15-16 year-olds needs a different (less purely rule-enforcement) posture than one aimed at 12-13 year-olds.

**Grades 11-12 (~16-18yo):** Highest-autonomy age band — unsupervised financial risk (in-game/app purchases, subscriptions signed up without parent knowledge), meeting people first encountered online in person, and AI-companion-chatbot overreliance/secrecy is an emerging 2025-documented behaviour (teens describing hiding chatbot relationships from parents in Reddit self-reports) ([Understanding Teen Overreliance on AI Companion Chatbots, arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.15783)).

**Most common vs highest-harm (synthesis, not a cited ranking):**
- *Most common*, low-harm-per-incident: exceeding screen-time limits, unapproved app/game downloads, watching restricted content, secondary/hidden social accounts.
- *Most common*, moderate-harm: sustained deception about who they're talking to, VPN/loophole-app circumvention of controls.
- *Lower-frequency but highest-harm*: sexting/non-consensual image sharing, meeting an online contact in person, sextortion exposure, secret AI-companion dependency.
- Note: cyberbullying research (Traits, Trends, and Trajectory of Tween and Teen Cyberbullies, [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7430539/)) treats bullying as its own behavioural trajectory, distinct from the parent-deception behaviours above — worth keeping separate in Phase 2 if the program scope includes "harm to others" not just "risk to self."

## Q2. Existing programs / competitive landscape

**Commercial parental-monitoring apps** (adjacent, not the same category — these are *surveillance* tools, not post-incident *reform/education* tools):
- Bark — $14/mo Premium, monitors 30+ platforms via content scanning; Bark Jr from $5/mo for filtering/screen-time only ([cybernews Aura vs Bark 2026](https://cybernews.com/best-parental-control-apps/bark-vs-aura/)).
- Canopy — $7.99-9.99/mo annual, AI-based real-time nudity/explicit-content blocking ([canopy.us Bark alternatives](https://canopy.us/blog/bark-alternatives/)).
- Aura — ~$12/mo, bundles identity-theft protection with parental controls (broader family-security play, not youth-education-specific).
- Qustodio — only "forever free" tier among major players.
None of these run a structured *post-incident completion course with parent sign-off and privilege restoration* — they are ongoing surveillance/filtering products. **This is the gap Aaron's concept sits in: nobody found sells a post-incident, time-boxed, completion-verified reform product.** That is a genuine competitive white space, not just an unresearched niche — worth treating as a real signal.

**School/nonprofit digital-citizenship curricula** (ongoing prevention, not post-incident): Common Sense Education's Digital Citizenship Curriculum, Google's "Be Internet Awesome," Fairfax County Public Schools' Shared Responsibility program, and in Canada, **MediaSmarts** — Canada's longest-running digital-literacy research and curriculum body since 1996, running the "Young Canadians in a Wireless World" study (20,000+ parents/teachers/students surveyed since 2000) ([MediaSmarts](https://mediasmarts.ca/), [Digital Media Literacy](https://mediasmarts.ca/digital-media-literacy)). These are curriculum-first, delivered by schools, not a parent-triggered post-incident product — but MediaSmarts is the credible Canadian-context source to cite/ground any DCC youth content in.

A 2025 systematic review (BERA/Wiley) documents an explicit field-level shift **"from safeguarding to critical digital citizenship"** — i.e., the education field itself is moving away from scare-tactic/rule-based approaches toward critical-thinking-based ones, which directly supports the restorative-framing recommendation below ([Estellés 2025, Review of Education](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rev3.70056)).

**Court-diversion / restorative programs (the closest real analogue to Aaron's concept):** at least 13 US states run formal sexting/cyberbullying diversion programs that let a first-time offending minor complete an education program instead of facing charges — structurally almost identical to Aaron's transgression → course → verified completion → reintegration pathway, just run by courts instead of parents:
- Ohio's C.A.R.D. program: 100+ youths completed since 2015, only 2 were subsequently charged — strong completion/non-recidivism signal for the diversion-education model generally ([CourtNewsOhio](https://www.courtnewsohio.gov/inDepth/2019/youthDiversions.asp)).
- New York, Florida, New Jersey run parallel remedial-education-in-lieu-of-prosecution programs ([criminaldefenselawyer.com NY sexting](https://www.criminaldefenselawyer.com/resources/teen-sexting-new-york.htm); [Florida SA14 juvenile diversion](https://sa14.fl.gov/juvenile-diversion/)).
- Wright County (Minnesota) ran a documented **restorative group conferencing** model specifically for a sexting incident — apology + empathy-building as the completion condition rather than a quiz/certificate ([cyberbullying.org, Restorative Group Conferencing and Sexting](https://cyberbullying.org/restorative-group-conferencing-and-sexting)).
- Academic tooling: "ApoloBot" is a documented research prototype exploring the *design space* for online restorative-justice tools with youth co-design input ([arXiv 2502.18861](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18861)).

**Who owns this space, honestly:** nobody owns the specific "parent-triggered, DIY, completion-verified, privilege-restoration" product category. The court-diversion programs are the closest structural precedent but are institutional/legal, not something a parent can self-administer at home for a non-legal household rule breach. That gap is real — but it also means there is no proof yet that a *self-serve, unsupervised-by-a-professional* version works; the diversion programs succeed partly because a counsellor/facilitator runs them, not just a course.

## Q3. Naming + framing evidence — recommendation: do NOT ship "Reform"

**Core finding — reintegrative vs disintegrative (stigmatizing) shaming, John Braithwaite:** Braithwaite's theory (tested empirically in multiple adolescent studies) distinguishes shaming that **condemns the act while reintegrating the person** (which reduces future rule-breaking) from shaming that **labels/stigmatizes the person's identity** (which increases it, by pushing the young person toward a deviant self-identity and away from the family/community that could otherwise pull them back). Empirical adolescent tests found "a durable negative effect of shaming on predatory delinquency" specifically for the *reintegrative* variant ([Hay 2001, An Exploratory Test of Braithwaite's Reintegrative Shaming Theory](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022427801038002002); [restorativejustice.org summary](https://restorativejustice.org/rj-archive/an-exploratory-test-of-braithwaites-reintegrative-shaming-theory/)). Restorative-justice practice literature is explicit that restorative approaches aim to "restore the victim and community while holding the offender accountable... rather than punishing the offender through a carceral approach" ([National Forum on the Prevention of Cyber Sexual Abuse — What is restorative justice?](https://nfpcsa.pubpub.org/pub/8vtlwzhl/release/1)).

**Field-level trend away from scare/punitive framing:** the 2025 systematic review found that "early internet-safety curricula relied heavily on scare tactics and sensationalized stories," and that the field is actively moving to "critical digital citizenship" — helping students explore complex questions rather than enforcing dos-and-don'ts ([Estellés 2025](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rev3.70056)).

**What this means for "Reform" specifically:** "Reform" is a penal-system word. Even used with good intent, it risks functioning as a **label applied to the kid** ("you are a person who needed reforming") rather than **a program applied to a situation** ("you broke a rule and are rebuilding trust"). That is precisely the disintegrative-shaming failure mode the research warns against. It also risks the product *reading* as punitive to the very audience (parents shopping for something that helps, not something that punishes) who would buy it — a practical go-to-market risk on top of the child-welfare risk.

**Recommendation:** keep the justice-system-analogy *structure* Aaron wants (breach → program → verified completion → reintegration — this is a legitimate, evidence-supported pathway, not just Aaron's private idea; it mirrors the real diversion-program model in Q2), but reframe the *name and voice* around **trust-rebuilding / earned-back privileges**, not punishment or "reform" of the person. Working direction (not final — Aaron's call): something like **"Trust Rebuild"**, **"Reset"**, **"Earned Back"**, or a DCC-branded variant (e.g. "DCC Trust Path"). Avoid identity-labeling words (reform, rehab, correction) in favour of act-and-relationship-focused words (trust, reset, rebuild, path). Final name is Aaron's decision — this is the evidence input, not an override.

## Q4. Parent-verification mechanic options (no-data-collection, local-only, ADR-0004)

DCC already has a working precedent to build on: `youth/course.js` implements a 25-question local quiz gated at a pass threshold, ending in a **printable certificate of completion** with the learner's name — no login, no server, no data leaves the device (confirmed by reading `C:\twobirds\digital-confidence\youth\course.js`). Any parent-verification mechanic for this program should extend that existing pattern rather than invent a new architecture. Options, roughly ascending in rigour/friction:

1. **Same-device ritual (lowest friction):** kid completes the course on the shared/kid's device; certificate renders on-screen; parent reviews it *with the kid present* and manually un-grounds them. No new mechanic needed — this is literally how the existing DCC youth certificate already works. Verification = a human moment, not a system.
2. **Printable certificate + physical signature line:** extend the existing certificate template to include a signature/date line, kid prints or shows it, parent signs physically. Zero data collection, keeps the "verified completion" as a real artifact the family can keep (also serves the "proof of completion" want in Q5).
3. **Locally-generated one-time code, entered by parent:** after passing the quiz, the page generates a short code purely client-side (e.g. derived from a local timestamp + a fixed salt, no server round-trip) and displays it to the kid; the kid tells the parent the code; the parent types it into a "verify" field on the *same page* to unlock a completion state stored in `localStorage` on that device only. This gives a slightly more tamper-resistant "the kid can't just click done themselves" step than option 1, while staying 100% local — no account, no server, no PII transmitted. Closest fit to ADR-0004.
4. **Cross-device handoff via QR code (still local-only):** completion state is encoded into a URL/QR code the kid shows the parent (e.g. on the parent's phone camera), parent scans it, lands on a "confirm completion" page that just flips a local flag on the parent's device — no server persistence, data only ever lives in the QR payload + two browsers' localStorage. Useful if parent and kid have separate devices, at the cost of more engineering complexity.
5. **What NOT to build:** anything requiring an account, email capture, or server-side completion ledger — that would need a new ADR to supersede ADR-0004 and is explicitly out of scope per the brief's sensitivity section.

Recommendation for Phase 2 scoping: start with option 2 (printable certificate + signature — near-zero build cost, reuses existing course.js) as the MVP, with option 3 as a "Phase 2.5" enhancement if Aaron wants more tamper-resistance than an honour-system signature.

## Q5. "Hardcore but not shaming" completion-course shape (rough)

Framing constraint from Q3: the course must feel like *real content the kid respects*, not a punishment worksheet — the diversion-program precedent (Q2) shows this works when the content is genuinely substantive (Ohio's C.A.R.D. covers real legal/relationship stakes, not a scolding). Rough shape, informed by the diversion-program structure + DCC's existing course.js pattern:

- **Length:** diversion programs typically run as a single structured session or a short multi-module block, not a multi-week commitment — a few hours total across modules feels right for a household (not legal-system) context. Existing DCC youth courses (`course-teen-intro.html`, `course-teen-advanced.html`) already have a module-based structure to extend.
- **Content pillars**, drawn from what actually happened (age-banded per Q1) rather than generic: (1) what happened and why it matters — not shame-framed, fact-framed (legal/relational stakes, e.g. what sextortion or image-sharing actually leads to); (2) the "why" behind the rule from the parent's perspective, in the kid's own words exercise; (3) a rebuild-trust action plan the kid writes themselves (restorative-justice literature's "apology as a condition for resolution, requiring empathy-building" — [cyberbullying.org](https://cyberbullying.org/restorative-group-conferencing-and-sexting)); (4) a forward-looking skills module matched to the specific transgression type (e.g., if it was VPN circumvention, a module on *why* the family rule exists, not just "don't use VPNs").
- **Assessment:** keep DCC's existing 25-question/pass-threshold quiz mechanic — it is already built, already privacy-safe, and mirrors the diversion-program pattern of "genuine comprehension check," not a token gesture.
- **Proof of completion:** printable certificate (already exists in course.js) extended with the family-verification step from Q4 option 2/3. Consider a designed "completion artifact" that reads as an accomplishment, not a rap sheet — visual language should match DCC's brand guidelines, not a mugshot aesthetic.
- **Explicitly avoid:** public/shareable completion (no social posting of "I got caught and did my course"), no ranking/scoring that could be compared between siblings or peers, no permanent record — completion state should be able to be cleared/reset by the family, consistent with ADR-0004's no-persistence stance.

## Q6. Monetization landscape (light)

Directly comparable "post-incident reform" products don't exist to benchmark against (see Q2), so this triangulates from adjacent categories:

- **Parental monitoring apps** (ongoing subscription, not one-time): Bark $5-14/mo, Canopy ~$8-10/mo, Aura ~$12/mo ([cybernews](https://cybernews.com/best-parental-control-apps/bark-vs-aura/), [canopy.us](https://canopy.us/blog/bark-alternatives/)). These are ongoing-surveillance pricing models — not a direct fit for a one-time, post-incident product.
- **Court/school diversion programs:** typically free or low-cost to families because they're publicly/institutionally funded (state/county programs) — no direct consumer price signal available; these are not sold to consumers so this data point mainly confirms there's no institutional price ceiling DCC needs to compete with in the household-direct market.
- **General online-course pricing trend (2025-2026):** the broader e-learning market is trending toward **one-time-purchase preference over subscription** — buyers are described as "subscription-fatigued" and willing to pay more upfront for lifetime access rather than commit to another monthly fee ([passion.io Cohort Course Pricing Guide](https://passion.io/blog/cohort-course-pricing-guide-one-time-payment-vs-subscription-models)). This favours a **one-time, per-incident purchase** model over a recurring subscription for Aaron's concept — it's naturally episodic (triggered by an incident), not an ongoing-use product, so a subscription model would be a mismatch to the use case anyway.
- **Plausible defensible model (not validated, a hypothesis for Phase 2):** one-time parent-pay fee per "reset" (single incident → single course → single verified completion), priced below the monthly cost of the surveillance-app category (i.e., positioned as a one-time intervention, not a recurring tool) — e.g. a $15-40 one-time price point would undercut a month of Bark/Canopy while being a believable "I'll pay this once to fix this specific problem" purchase. This is a hypothesis to validate with real parents in Phase 2, not a researched number — no source prices this exact category.
- **School-license angle:** not researched in depth this pass — flagged as an open question below rather than guessed at.

## Open questions for Aaron (Phase 2 shaping)

1. **Name/tone:** does the evidence in Q3 (reintegrative-shaming research + field trend away from punitive framing) move you off "Reform," or do you want to keep it deliberately (e.g. as a private/internal codename vs a public-facing name)? Recommendation above is a working direction, not a decision.
2. **Scope of "transgression":** does this cover the full range in Q1 (screen-time violations through sexting/legal-risk incidents), or is DCC's version scoped narrower (e.g. deliberately excluding anything that's actually a legal/safeguarding matter that should go to a real diversion program or professional, not a self-serve DCC course)? This is a real line — Q2's diversion programs work partly *because* a trained facilitator is involved for the highest-harm incidents; a self-serve product may not be appropriate for those cases and DCC should be explicit about that boundary (e.g. a "this needs a real adult professional, not an app" redirect for sextortion/legal-risk incidents).
3. **Verification rigour (Q4):** is the honour-system printable-certificate-plus-signature (option 2) enough, or does Aaron want the extra tamper-resistance of a locally-generated code (option 3) from day one?
4. **Content authorship:** who writes the age-banded, incident-specific course content — DCC in-house (per SKILLS-INSTALL/sovereignty norms) or with outside expert/counsellor review given the sensitivity class? The brief's own sensitivity section implies expert review is expected before any build — worth deciding who that reviewer is now, not at build time.
5. **Pricing model (Q6):** one-time per-incident fee is the research-favoured direction — does Aaron want to validate that price point with a handful of real parents before Phase 2 design, or treat it as directional and revisit at launch?
6. **Relationship to existing DCC youth courses:** is this a new standalone product/section, or an extension of the existing `youth/course-teen-*.html` + `course.js` infrastructure (which would reuse rather than duplicate build effort)? Given course.js already implements 90% of the Q4/Q5 mechanic, extension looks like the lower-effort path — flagging for Aaron's call, not deciding it here.
7. **"Un-grounding" as the trigger:** Aaron's original framing ties the course strictly to restoring privileges after a *breach*. Should DCC also offer this as a *proactive* (non-punitive, opt-in) family digital-agreement course — same content, different entry point — to broaden the audience beyond "kids who got caught," given the framing research suggests avoiding an identity/audience that reads as exclusively remedial?
