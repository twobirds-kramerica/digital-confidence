Module 12: Getting the Help You Deserve

What you will learn: How to navigate customer service, advocate for yourself when dealing with tech support, know your rights as a consumer in Canada, and stay safe when someone offers to help with your device remotely.

You Deserve Good Help

Getting help with technology — or with a bill, a charge, or a complaint — should not feel like a battle. But it sometimes does. You wait on hold, press endless buttons, and still feel like you did not get what you needed.

This module is about changing that. You have rights as a consumer in Canada. You have resources available to you. And you have the right to ask for help at your own pace, in plain language, until you fully understand what is happening. No one should rush you, confuse you on purpose, or pressure you into anything.

You deserve good help. This module shows you how to get it.

📞 Section 1: The Automated Phone Maze

When you call a company and get an automated system (called an IVR — Interactive Voice Response), it can feel like there is no way to reach a real person. The system asks questions, gives you options, and loops back to the start if it does not understand you. It can feel very frustrating.

Here are practical tips that work on most automated phone systems:

1 Say "representative" or "agent" clearly and calmly into the phone. Many systems are programmed to transfer you to a real person when they hear these words.
2 Press 0 (zero) on your keypad. This works on many systems and often transfers you to a live operator immediately.
3 Say "I need help" — this phrase triggers a transfer on some systems, especially banking and utility companies.
4 If the system keeps looping or not understanding you, hang up and try again at a different time of day. Early morning (right when they open) and Tuesday–Thursday midday are usually the quietest times.
Dorothy's Discovery

Dorothy called her bank about a charge she did not recognise. She spent 15 minutes pressing buttons before remembering to just say "representative" clearly. She was connected immediately.

Helpful Hint

Before you call, write down your account number, the amount in question, and the date it appeared. Having this ready means the call goes faster and you sound confident — which helps.

⚖️ Section 2: Your Rights as a Consumer

In Canada, you have rights when things go wrong with a company. You do not have to simply accept a bad outcome. Here are the key organisations that can help if a company is not treating you fairly:

1 CCTS — Commissioner for Complaints for Telecom-television Services
For problems with your phone or internet provider that the company has not resolved after 30 days.
Website: ccts-cprst.ca — Free. No cost to file a complaint.
2 OBSI — Ombudsman for Banking Services and Investments
For banking or investment problems that your bank or financial institution will not resolve.
Website: obsi.ca — Free. Independent. Available across Canada.
3 Consumer Protection Ontario
For consumer complaints in Ontario — purchases, contracts, door-to-door sales, and more.
Website: consumerprotection.on.ca
Phone: 1-800-889-9768 (free call within Ontario)
Keep a Call Log

Keep a log of every call you make — date, time, who you spoke to, and what was agreed. This is your evidence if you need to escalate. A small notebook kept beside your phone works perfectly.

Important Safety Rule

Legitimate customer service agents will never ask for your full password, PIN, or Social Insurance Number (SIN) over the phone. If they do — hang up immediately. Call the company back using the number on the back of your card or from the official website.

👨‍👩‍👧 Section 3: When a Family Member Wants to Help

Having a loved one help with technology is wonderful — but it can sometimes feel rushed or confusing. Your son or daughter may know exactly what to do and move very quickly. You may feel embarrassed to ask them to slow down. This is very common.

It is perfectly fine to say: "I appreciate your help, but I would like to try it myself first. Can we go through it together slowly?"

A good helper will always say yes. And if they are in a hurry, that is fine too — you can ask them to walk you through it once slowly, and then you can practise on your own later.

💛

Asking for help at your own pace is a skill, not a weakness. You deserve to understand what is happening on your device. Every step you understand is a step you own.

A Helpful Phrase

When a family member is helping and moving too fast, try: "Can you do that one more time? I want to try it myself." Most people are happy to repeat steps — they just do not realise how quickly they move.

🖥️ Section 4: Remote Help — Safe and Unsafe

Sometimes technical helpers — whether family, a computer store, or a support line you called — need to see your screen remotely. This means they can view and sometimes control your phone or computer from another location over the internet.

This can be completely safe in the right circumstances. The key question is: Did you contact them first?

When Remote Help Is Safe

Remote access is safe when YOU contact a trusted service and YOU choose to begin the session. Safe options include Apple's built-in Screen Sharing feature (ask your helper to explain it step by step before you begin) and Quick Assist on Windows computers. If you are not sure how to start it, ask the person helping you to walk you through the steps slowly before anything happens.

Critical Warning — This Is a Very Common Scam

Never allow remote access if someone called YOU unexpectedly — even if they claim to be from Apple, Microsoft, your bank, the Canada Revenue Agency, or a computer repair company. This is one of the most common and damaging scams targeting seniors. They may sound very convincing and official. Hang up anyway.

🛑

The Three-Second Rule for Remote Access

Before allowing anyone to access your device remotely: Stop. Think. Did you contact them first? If the answer is no — hang up. Do not let anyone pressure you. A real company will never call you out of the blue and ask to access your device.
