Family Conversation Guide: Talking About Digital Safety

This guide is for adult children, grandchildren, and caregivers who want to have productive conversations about online safety with older family members — without being condescending, alarming, or dismissive of their independence.

The conversations that work best are ones that start from respect. Your parent or grandparent has managed their finances, raised families, and navigated complex challenges for decades. Digital safety is a new domain for everyone — including you — and approaching it as something you are figuring out together, rather than something you are teaching them, makes a significant difference.

Why These Conversations Often Go Wrong

The "Don't Click Anything" approach
Telling a senior to avoid clicking anything online is not safety advice — it is paralysis advice. It creates anxiety, reduces independence, and does not teach them to make informed decisions. The goal is confident, discerning use of technology — not avoidance.
Taking over their device without asking
Grabbing the phone or tablet to show what to do — without explaining what you are doing — builds dependence rather than skill. It can also feel like a loss of control. Sit beside them, narrate your steps, and let them do the tapping when possible.
Leading with horror stories
Opening with "I read about a grandmother who lost $80,000 to a scammer" may create fear without the knowledge to act on it. Fear without context tends to produce avoidance, shame (if something has already happened), or defensive dismissal.

What to Say Instead

❌ "You really need to be more careful — you could get scammed."

✅ "These scams are getting more sophisticated — I almost fell for one the other day. Can we talk about what to look for?"

❌ "Don't click on things you don't recognise."

✅ "If an email creates a sense of urgency, like 'act now or lose your account,' that's usually the sign — let's look at one together."

❌ "Just call me before you do anything online."

✅ "The 3-second pause works really well — if something feels off, wait three seconds and ask yourself if you were expecting this."

❌ "Why did you click on that?"

✅ "These things are designed by professionals to fool people — let's figure out together what to do next."

Practical Things to Do Together

Set up a family code word (15 minutes)
Agree on a word or phrase that only your family knows — to be used if anyone calls claiming to be a family member in distress. Ask for the code word. If they do not know it, it is a scam. This one step addresses the grandparent scam and AI voice clone scams directly.
Set up iCloud Backup (10 minutes)
Settings → Your Name → iCloud → iCloud Backup → Turn on. This protects their photos and data. If their device is ever lost, stolen, or breaks, everything can be restored.
Set up Medical ID (10 minutes)
Health app → Profile photo → Medical ID → Edit. Add emergency contacts, medical conditions, and medications. Turn on "Show When Locked." This is visible to first responders without the passcode.
Explore the Digital Confidence Centre together (30 minutes)
The DCC modules are designed to be completed independently, but going through Module 2 (The Security Shield) together — pausing to discuss examples — is one of the most effective ways to build a shared vocabulary around scam awareness without lectures or fear-mongering.
Check their app and software updates (5 minutes)
Settings → General → Software Update (for iPad/iPhone updates). App Store → Profile picture → Update All (for app updates). Turn on automatic updates. This requires no ongoing attention and provides significant security benefits.

If They Have Already Been Scammed

Important: Shame and self-blame are extremely common responses for scam victims — and they are unfounded. These criminals are professionals. The response that helps is matter-of-fact and focused on next steps, not on how it happened.
The right first words
Start with: "This is not your fault. These people are professional criminals. Let's figure out what to do next." Then move immediately to practical steps — calling the bank if money was lost, changing passwords, reporting to CAFC.
Practical steps after a scam
  1. If money was involved — call the bank immediately (number on back of card).
  2. Change the password on any affected accounts.
  3. If a device was accessed — contact a local repair shop or trusted tech person to check it.
  4. Report to CAFC: 1-888-495-8501 (helps protect others).
  5. Consider a credit freeze if significant personal information was shared — Equifax 1-800-465-7166 and TransUnion 1-800-663-9980.
