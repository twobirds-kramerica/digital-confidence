# Creating a Strong Password
**Module:** Module 3: Passwords & Biometrics
**Duration Target:** 3-4 minutes
**Audience:** Ontario seniors aged 70+

---

## SCRIPT

**[OPEN]**
[NARRATOR] Welcome back. You're doing great.

**[VISUAL CUE: Warm title card — "Creating a Strong Password"]**
[NARRATOR] Passwords are the keys to your online life. Your email, your bank, your photos — all protected by passwords. Today we're going to look at what makes a password strong, what makes one weak, and an easy method for creating passwords you can actually remember.

**[VISUAL CUE: Show a locked door with a small, flimsy padlock vs a large, heavy-duty lock]**
[NARRATOR] Think of a password like a lock on your front door. A weak password is like a lock that any key could open. A strong password is a deadbolt — it takes effort and the right key. The good news is that making a strong password is easier than you might think.

**[VISUAL CUE: Show common weak passwords — "password123", "1234", "fluffy" — with red X marks]**
[NARRATOR] Weak passwords are things like "password," your name, your birthday, or a single word from the dictionary. These are the first things anyone trying to break in would guess. If a password is easy for you to remember because it's so simple, it's easy for someone else to guess, too.

**[VISUAL CUE: Show a "passphrase" concept — four random words strung together]**
[NARRATOR] Here is the method I recommend. Instead of a password — one complex word — use a passphrase — four simple words in a row. Something like "purple maple kettle rain." Four ordinary words that you can picture in your mind but that nobody would ever guess.

**[VISUAL CUE: Animate the four words appearing — "purple maple kettle rain" — with a green checkmark]**
[NARRATOR] "Purple maple kettle rain." That's 22 characters long. It has no personal connection to you. A computer trying to guess it would take millions of years to work through all the possibilities. And yet, you can easily picture a purple maple tree in the rain next to a kettle. Your brain holds onto images much better than random symbols.

**[VISUAL CUE: Show a sentence being turned into a passphrase — "I love tea in Ontario" → "iloveteainOntario1"]**
[NARRATOR] Another approach: take a sentence that means something to you and use the first letter of each word. "I drink two cups of tea every morning" becomes "Id2coteM" — and you can add a number or symbol to make it even stronger. The key is that the sentence lives in your memory, but what's written as the password makes no sense to anyone else.

**[VISUAL CUE: Text on screen — "Never use the same password for two different accounts"]**
[NARRATOR] Here is one of the most important rules: never use the same password on two different accounts. If one website gets hacked and your password is stolen, criminals will immediately try that password on your email, your bank, and dozens of other sites. Different accounts need different passwords.

**[VISUAL CUE: Show a physical notebook with passwords written in it — sitting in a desk drawer]**
[NARRATOR] Writing passwords down is actually fine — as long as that notebook is kept safely at home, away from your computer. Not on a sticky note on your screen. Not in a note on your iPad. In a little notebook, in a drawer. The risk of someone breaking into your home and finding your password notebook is much lower than the risk of using the same weak password everywhere online.

**[VISUAL CUE: Show iCloud Keychain logo]**
[NARRATOR] In our next video, we'll look at iCloud Keychain — a tool that remembers all your passwords for you so you only have to remember one. But first, it helps to understand what a strong password looks like, which is what we've covered today.

**[VISUAL CUE: Summary card — "Long + random + unique = strong password"]**
[NARRATOR] To summarise: long is better than complicated. Four random words beat one complex word with symbols. And never reuse passwords. You now know more about passwords than most people.

**[CLOSE]**
[NARRATOR] Remember the 3-Second Rule: if something feels wrong, stop, breathe, and wait three seconds before tapping anything. You can always come back to something, but you can't un-tap a link. See you in the next video.
