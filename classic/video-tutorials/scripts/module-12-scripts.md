[VOICEOVER]: "Welcome to Module 12 of the Digital Confidence Centre — Getting the Help You Deserve. This module covers your rights as a consumer and how to get effective help with technology. ### MAIN CONTENT

**SEGMENT 1: The Consumer Protection Act — Your Foundation**
[VOICEOVER]: "Ontario's Consumer Protection Act is the main law that protects you when you make purchases. Key protections that apply to digital products and online shopping include: the right to a receipt for any purchase, the right to cancel most contracts within 10 days if you weren't given all the required information, the right to a refund if something you paid for isn't delivered, and strong protections against unfair business practices and misleading advertising. Service Ontario's website has plain-language guides to all of these rights."
[VISUAL CUE]: Ontario government website shown. Consumer Protection Act summary. Key rights listed with icons.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Consumer Protection Act: 10-day cancellation right · refund if not delivered · receipt required

**SEGMENT 2: App Store and Digital Purchase Refund Rights**
[VOICEOVER]: "If you purchase an app or an in-app item on the App Store and it doesn't work as described, Apple offers refunds. To request one, go to reportaproblem.apple.com from your iPad's browser — or go to the App Store, tap your profile icon, purchase history, and then report a problem on the specific purchase. Apple's refund policy for digital purchases is more generous than many people realise — especially for purchases made in the last 14 days. You can also contact Apple Support directly."
[VISUAL CUE]: App Store → profile icon → purchase history → Report a Problem. Also: reportaproblem.apple.com in Safari.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: App Store refund: reportaproblem.apple.com · Or App Store → profile → purchase history

**SEGMENT 3: Online Shopping — Your Return and Refund Rights**
[VOICEOVER]: "When you buy something online from a Canadian retailer, you have the right to cancel or return it in most circumstances. Under the Consumer Protection Act, online purchases include a 7-day review period for many goods — the seller must provide written disclosure of their return policy, and if they don't, your right to return is extended. Keep every order confirmation email as your proof of purchase. If a retailer refuses a reasonable return, the next step is to file a chargeback with your credit card provider — your bank can reverse the charge if the retailer hasn't honoured the transaction."
[VISUAL CUE]: Order confirmation email highlighted. Chargeback request process at bank — illustrated.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Keep all order confirmation emails · Refused return? Contact your credit card company for a chargeback

**SEGMENT 4: Subscription Cancellation Rights**
[VOICEOVER]: "Subscriptions — monthly charges for apps, streaming services, or software — can sometimes be difficult to cancel. In Ontario, you have the right to cancel a continuous service contract by giving notice. For digital subscriptions on your iPad, you can cancel directly in Settings — your name — Subscriptions, as we covered in Module 4. If a service makes cancellation unreasonably difficult, document your attempts and contact the company in writing. If you're still charged after cancelling, your credit card provider can dispute the charge."
[VISUAL CUE]: Settings → Subscriptions → Cancel Subscription shown. Written cancellation notice graphic.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Cancel iPad subscriptions: Settings → your name → Subscriptions · Keep written record of cancellation

**SEGMENT 5: Telecom Rights — CRTC and Your Phone Provider**
[VOICEOVER]: "If you have a mobile phone or internet plan, you're protected by the CRTC — Canada's telecommunications regulator. The Wireless Code of Conduct gives you the right to cancel a two-year contract after two years with no penalty, the right to have your contract clearly explained in plain language, and the right to unlock your phone for free from your carrier. If your provider overcharges you or doesn't honour your contract, you can escalate to the Commission for Complaints for Telecom-television Services — CCTS — at ccts.ca. This is free and is a powerful escalation path."
[VISUAL CUE]: CRTC logo. CCTS website shown. Wireless Code summary points.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Telecom dispute? CCTS: ccts.ca — free, independent resolution for Canadians

### COMMON MISTAKES (1 minute)
[VOICEOVER]: "Two things people don't realise they're entitled to. First: a refund for a subscription you didn't realise had renewed. If an annual subscription renewed without adequate notice and you didn't intend to continue, contact the company and ask for a refund. Apple's policy explicitly allows refunds for recent unexpected renewals. Second: a credit card chargeback. Many people think a chargeback is aggressive or unusual — it's a standard consumer protection that all credit card providers offer, free of charge, when a legitimate purchase goes wrong."
[VISUAL CUE]: Chargeback form at bank — normalised, presented as a standard process. Apple subscription renewal refund path.

Two: app and digital purchase refunds are available at reportaproblem.apple.com. Three: keep order confirmations — chargebacks are your backup. Four: telecom disputes go to ccts.ca — free and effective."
[VISUAL CUE]: Four-point recap list.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: 10-day cancel right · reportaproblem.apple.com · Keep receipts, use chargebacks · ccts.ca for telecom

## Video 2 of 3: Getting Good Tech Support
**Target Duration:** 7 minutes
**Difficulty:** Beginner

### HOOK (First 15 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "Getting tech support shouldn't feel like asking for a favour. Whether it's Apple, your phone carrier, or your internet provider — you paid for their product, and getting it to work properly is a reasonable expectation. Today we'll make sure you know exactly how to get help efficiently — and how to spot fake tech support scams."

### INTRODUCTION (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "In this video, we'll cover how to contact Apple Support effectively, how to get help from your phone carrier, community tech help resources in Ontario, and the very important topic of how to recognise fake tech support scams — which specifically target older adults."
[VISUAL CUE]: Apple Support icon. Title card.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Module 12 · Video 2 of 3: Getting Good Tech Support

---

### MAIN CONTENT

**SEGMENT 1: Apple Support — Multiple Ways to Get Help**
[VOICEOVER]: "Apple offers several ways to get support. The Apple Support app — downloadable free from the App Store — lets you describe your problem, schedule a call or chat with an Apple advisor, and book a Genius Bar appointment at an Apple Store. The number for Apple Support Canada is 1-800-MY-APPLE. The Apple Support website at apple.com/ca/support has an extensive library of how-to guides. If you have an Apple Store nearby — Toronto, Ottawa, London, Kingston — the Genius Bar is free for advice and diagnostics."
[VISUAL CUE]: Apple Support app shown. Genius Bar booking flow. Apple Canada support number displayed large.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Apple Support Canada: 1-800-MY-APPLE · apple.com/ca/support · Free Genius Bar at Apple Stores

**SEGMENT 2: Preparing Before You Call Support**
[VOICEOVER]: "A few minutes of preparation makes a support call much more efficient. Know your Apple ID email address — you'll almost certainly be asked for it. Know your device model — go to Settings, then General, then About, to find your iPad model name. Write down a clear description of the problem: when it started, what you were doing when it happened, and what you've already tried. Have your iPad charged and in front of you during the call. This preparation saves time and helps the advisor understand your situation immediately."
[VISUAL CUE]: Settings → General → About → model name shown. Notes with preparation checklist graphic.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Before calling: Apple ID email · device model · problem description · iPad charged and in hand

**SEGMENT 3: Community Tech Help Resources in Ontario**
[VOICEOVER]: "Many Ontario communities have free or low-cost technology help available for older adults. Public libraries often run digital literacy programs and one-on-one help sessions. Seniors' centres and community centres frequently host drop-in tech help sessions. The Digital Literacy Exchange Program, funded by the federal government, provides free technology education across Canada. To find resources in your area, contact your local library, call your municipal seniors' services line, or visit the Digital Confidence Centre website."
[VISUAL CUE]: Library digital literacy session graphic. Community centre seniors group. DCC website shown.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Free tech help: your local library · seniors centre · call your municipal seniors' services

**SEGMENT 4: Carrier Support — Bell, Rogers, Telus, Koodo, and Others**
[VOICEOVER]: "If your problem is with your phone plan, internet, or a device you purchased through your carrier, contact the carrier's support line. The key is to have your account number or the phone number on the account ready, as well as the device model if relevant. Bell's customer service is 1-866-310-BELL. Rogers is 1-888-764-3771. Telus is 1-866-558-2273. Koodo and Virgin are subsidiaries of Telus and Bell respectively — you can find their support numbers on their websites or the back of your monthly bill."
[VISUAL CUE]: Bell, Rogers, Telus, Koodo logos shown briefly. Monthly bill back highlighting customer service number.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Carrier support: on your monthly bill · or search "[carrier name] support Canada"

**SEGMENT 5: Fake Tech Support Scams — How to Spot Them**
[VOICEOVER]: "Fake tech support is one of the most common scams targeting older adults. It works like this: you get a phone call or a pop-up on your screen claiming that your device has a virus or has been hacked, and that you need to call a number immediately. The 'technician' then asks for remote access to your device — and once they have it, they install malware, steal information, or demand payment to 'fix' the problem. The rule to remember: Apple, Microsoft, and your internet provider will never call you unsolicited to tell you your device has a problem. If it's unsolicited, it's a scam."
[VISUAL CUE]: Fake tech support pop-up shown with red flags annotated. "They will never call you" message displayed boldly.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Unsolicited "tech support" call or pop-up = SCAM · Real companies do not call you about viruses

---

### COMMON MISTAKES (1 minute)
[VOICEOVER]: "Two mistakes when dealing with tech support. First: accepting remote access to your device from anyone you contacted first. If you called Apple, your carrier, or a certified technician and they request remote access through a legitimate app — this can be legitimate. But never grant remote access to someone who called you or sent you a pop-up. Second: not writing down the case or reference number at the end of a support call. If you need to follow up or escalate, having the case number makes the process much faster."
[VISUAL CUE]: Legitimate vs. unsolicited remote access scenarios. Case number being written down at end of support call.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Write down the case number at the end of every support call

---

### RECAP (30 seconds)

Two: prepare before calling — Apple ID, device model, problem description. Three: free tech help at libraries and seniors' centres. Four: unsolicited tech support calls or pop-ups are always scams."
[VISUAL CUE]: Four-point recap list.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: 1-800-MY-APPLE · Prepare before calling · Free help at library · Unsolicited contact = scam

## Video 3 of 3: When and How to Escalate a Complaint
**Target Duration:** 6 minutes
**Difficulty:** Intermediate

### HOOK (First 15 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "You've called customer service three times. You've been put on hold, transferred, and promised a callback that never came. When the front line isn't solving your problem, there are escalation paths that genuinely work — and they're yours to use."

### INTRODUCTION (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "In this final video of Module 12, we'll cover how to escalate a complaint when standard customer service isn't resolving your issue. This applies to tech companies, retailers, banks, and service providers. There are specific steps that consistently get results."
[VISUAL CUE]: Escalation ladder graphic. Title card.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Module 12 · Video 3 of 3: Escalating a Complaint Effectively

---

### MAIN CONTENT

**SEGMENT 1: Document Everything First**
[VOICEOVER]: "Before escalating, write down: the date and time of every contact you've made, the name of every person you spoke with, what was promised and what was actually done, and your case or reference numbers. This documentation does two things: it demonstrates that you've made a genuine effort to resolve the problem through normal channels, and it gives you specifics — dates, names, promises — that are hard to dismiss. Keep this in a simple notebook or a note in the Notes app on your iPad."
[VISUAL CUE]: Notes app on iPad with a simple log of support calls: date, name, case number, what was promised.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Log every call: date · name · case number · what was promised

**SEGMENT 2: Asking for a Supervisor**
[VOICEOVER]: "The most direct escalation step is asking to speak with a supervisor or manager. Be calm and specific: 'I've called three times about this issue and haven't had it resolved. I'd like to speak with a supervisor, please.' Don't apologise for asking — this is a standard and reasonable request. If the representative says a supervisor will call you back, get a specific timeframe and a reference number. If the callback doesn't happen in that timeframe, call again with your case number and reference the missed callback."
[VISUAL CUE]: Call scenario: politely requesting a supervisor. Writing down callback timeframe and reference number.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: "I'd like to speak with a supervisor, please" — calm, direct, reasonable

**SEGMENT 3: Writing a Formal Complaint Letter**
[VOICEOVER]: "If phone calls aren't working, a formal written complaint — by email or post — is often more effective. Address it to 'Customer Relations' or the company's official complaints address, which is usually on their website. State clearly: what you purchased, what the problem is, what you've already done to resolve it, and what resolution you're asking for — a refund, a replacement, an apology in writing. Give a deadline: 'I would appreciate a response within 14 business days.' Written complaints create a paper trail and are taken more seriously than phone calls."
[VISUAL CUE]: Email draft in iPad Mail app addressed to Customer Relations. Key elements of the letter highlighted.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Written complaint: what you bought · the problem · what you've tried · what resolution you want

**SEGMENT 4: External Escalation — Ombudsmen and Regulators**
[VOICEOVER]: "If internal escalation fails, several external bodies can help in Canada. For banks: the Ombudsman for Banking Services and Investments at obsi.ca handles unresolved banking complaints — free, independent, and binding for smaller banks. For telecommunications: CCTS at ccts.ca, as mentioned in Video 1. For general consumer issues: the Better Business Bureau at bbb.org/ca logs your complaint publicly. For Ontario-specific issues: the Ontario Consumer Protection Branch at consumer.gov.on.ca."
[VISUAL CUE]: Four logos/websites shown: OBSI, CCTS, BBB Canada, Ontario Consumer Protection.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Banking: obsi.ca · Telecom: ccts.ca · General: bbb.org/ca · Ontario: consumer.gov.on.ca

**SEGMENT 5: Social Media as an Escalation Tool**
[VOICEOVER]: "Here's an escalation path that many people don't know about: publicly posting about an unresolved issue on social media — especially Twitter/X or Facebook — can prompt surprisingly fast responses from larger companies. Most major companies monitor their social media accounts for complaints and have dedicated teams to respond quickly to public issues. If you post, be factual and professional: state what happened, what you've already tried, and what you're asking for. This is not about shaming — it's about being heard."
[VISUAL CUE]: Social media post graphic showing a concise, professional complaint post. Company response appearing below.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Social media complaint: factual + professional + specific request = fast company response

---

### COMMON MISTAKES (1 minute)
[VOICEOVER]: "Two escalation mistakes. First: escalating too aggressively too fast. A calm, clear request for a supervisor on the second or third contact is appropriate. Threatening legal action on the first call is usually counterproductive. Work through the escalation levels systematically. Second: giving up before exhausting your options. Many legitimate complaints go unresolved simply because the person stopped pursuing them. The escalation paths in this video — ombudsmen, regulators, consumer protection — exist specifically for cases where the front line has failed."
[VISUAL CUE]: Escalation ladder graphic with steps: front line → supervisor → written complaint → external regulator.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Work through levels calmly — escalate to the next level if the previous one fails

---

### RECAP (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "Today's key points. One: document every contact — date, name, case number, promises made. Two: ask for a supervisor by name, calmly and directly. Three: a written complaint with a deadline often achieves what phone calls didn't. Four: external escalation paths — OBSI for banking, CCTS for telecom, Ontario Consumer Protection — are free and effective."
[VISUAL CUE]: Four-point recap list.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Document everything · Request supervisor · Written complaint · External regulators: obsi.ca, ccts.ca
