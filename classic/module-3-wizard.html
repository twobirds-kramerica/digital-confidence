Module 3: Passwords and Biometrics

What you will learn: How to make passwords that are easy for you to remember and impossible for strangers to guess — plus how Face ID works and why it is safe to use.

Robert's Story

Robert, 68, used the same password for everything: the name of his first dog and the year he was born. It was easy to remember, and he had used it for years.

One morning, he got an email saying someone had signed into his email account from a city he had never visited. A stranger had guessed his password — and used it to access three other accounts in the same hour.

Robert had to spend an entire Saturday resetting passwords and calling his bank. It was stressful, embarrassing, and completely preventable.

Tell me more — how do strangers guess passwords?

Hackers do not personally try to guess your password. They use programs that automatically try thousands of common choices — pet names, sports teams, birth years, sports heroes. A password that feels personal to you is often easy for a machine to crack in minutes.

The good news: a few simple techniques make your passwords nearly unguessable, even by the fastest computer program.

The Passphrase Method

Instead of a password like "Fido1954", try a passphrase: three or four ordinary words strung together with dashes.

For example: purple-kettle-garden-1954

That passphrase is 26 characters long. It would take a program millions of years to guess by brute force. And you can remember it because you can picture it: a purple kettle in a garden.

Tell me more — what makes a strong passphrase?

Aim for at least 16 characters. Use three to four unrelated words. Add a number or symbol somewhere — at the beginning, the end, or in between. The words do not need to make sense together. In fact, the stranger the combination, the stronger the passphrase.

"cloud-bicycle-Niagara-7" is excellent. "MyDogFido1954" is not. The length is what protects you — not the symbols.

Face ID Is Safe to Use

Face ID on iPhones and iPads lets you unlock your device using your face instead of typing a password. Many people worry this means Apple is storing a photo of their face somewhere. It does not work that way.

Your device creates a mathematical map of your face and stores it only on your device — never in the cloud, never on a server. Using Face ID is safer than typing a PIN where someone might see your screen.

Tell me more about how Face ID compares to a PIN

Face ID has a failure rate of about 1 in 1,000,000 — meaning a stranger who picks up your phone has roughly a one-in-a-million chance of unlocking it with their face.

A four-digit PIN, by contrast, can be guessed in at most 10,000 tries. And thieves who steal phones often watch people enter their PIN first, before taking the device. If you have Face ID available, turning it on is one of the best security improvements you can make today.

Two Small Tools That Make a Big Difference

Two-factor authentication (2FA) means a website texts you a code when you try to sign in. Even if someone steals your password, they cannot sign in without also having your phone. Turn it on for your email, your bank, and your Apple ID.

iCloud Keychain is a password manager that is already on your iPhone or iPad. When you sign in to a website, it offers to save the password. When you return, it fills it in automatically. You do not need to buy or install anything.

Tell me more — where do I find 2FA and iCloud Keychain?

For 2FA: look in the "Settings" of each account you want to protect. Search for "Two-factor authentication" or "Two-step verification." Most banks and email providers offer it.

For iCloud Keychain: on your iPhone or iPad, go to Settings, tap your name at the top, then tap iCloud, then tap Passwords. Ask someone you trust to help you turn it on if you are unsure — it takes about two minutes.

Your Passwords Are Stronger Now

Here is what you learned in this lesson:

  • A passphrase — three or four ordinary words with dashes — is far stronger than a short password with symbols, and easier to remember.
  • Face ID stores your data only on your device. It is safe to use and harder to steal than a PIN.
  • Two-factor authentication means a thief cannot use your password alone — they would also need your phone.
  • iCloud Keychain, already on your device, can remember your passwords so you only need to remember one.

One thing you can do today: Change one important password to a passphrase. Pick three words you can picture together, add a number, and write it down somewhere safe while you get used to it. Even one account protected this way is a win.
