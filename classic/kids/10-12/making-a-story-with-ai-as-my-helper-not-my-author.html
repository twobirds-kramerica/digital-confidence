Making a story with AI as my helper, not my author

When you write 7 of 10 lines, the story is yours. How to use AI as a creative tool while keeping ownership of what you make.

Creative Making ⏱ 20 min 💻 Device optional

The big idea

AI can suggest a sentence. You decide whether to use it, change it, or throw it away and write something better. That decision — made 7 times out of 10 — is what makes the story yours.

Before you use any AI chat tool

Most AI chat tools — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — have a minimum age of 13 to create an account. This activity is designed so you can do it without creating any account at all — the AI sentence options are already included below as pre-written choices.

If you want to try it with a real AI tool, use a parent or caregiver's account in a supervised session — not your own. That also applies to school projects.

The My Line / AI Line Rule

The rule is simple: in a 10-line story, you write at least 7 lines yourself. AI can contribute up to 3 — and even those you can rewrite, reject, or use only as a starting point. The point is that the core creative decisions stay with you.

My Line / AI Line Rule

Minimum 7 of 10 lines: yours.

Your lines (7 minimum)

Lines 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10 — the opening, the middle, and the ending. The decisions that shape the story. You write these.

AI contribution (3 maximum)

Lines 3, 6, 9 — you can use a pre-written AI suggestion, rewrite it, combine it with your own idea, or skip it entirely. Your choice.

Why 7 out of 10? Because creative ownership isn't about whether AI touched the work — it's about whether you made the meaningful decisions. Setting up the character, building the conflict, and writing the ending are the choices that define a story. If those are yours, the story is yours.

AI-assisted vs AI-generated — knowing the difference

This is a distinction that matters — for school projects, for your creative identity, and for how you talk about work you've made.

✅ AI-assisted

You made the core work. AI helped with parts — suggested a sentence, offered an option, corrected a spelling.

Examples: You wrote 7 of 10 story lines. You used spell-check. You used auto-correct. You asked AI for one synonym and chose one.

You can say: "I wrote this with some AI suggestions."

⚠️ AI-generated

AI made most or all of the work. You provided the prompt and accepted the output with little or no change.

Examples: You typed "write me a story about a robot" and submitted what came back. You asked AI to write the introduction of your essay.

You cannot say: "I wrote this." You can say: "This was generated by AI."

Why the distinction matters: Teachers will ask what you contributed. Employers will ask what you built. Other creators will ask what ideas were yours. Being able to clearly answer — "I wrote these parts; AI helped with these" — is a skill that will matter more as AI tools become more common, not less.

The target is 7 or above. If you're at 4 or 5, go back and rewrite two more lines from scratch — change the direction, add a detail only you would think of, give the character a reaction that came from your imagination. Then re-count.

One safety thing to know

Important

Real AI writing tools — the ones built by reputable companies for general use — do not ask you personal questions about your home, your school, your parents, or where you live.

If a tool that calls itself "AI" asks you personal questions, offers to be your friend, or asks you to keep your conversations secret — that is not how legitimate AI writing tools work. Tell a caregiver immediately.

AI writing tools are text tools. They help with sentences. They do not need to know your name, your age, where you go to school, or who your friends are.

Prompt A

Prompt B

When you're done: count your lines. How many are genuinely yours? Mark your score on the ownership meter. If you're at 7 or above — the story is yours.
After the activity: Can you explain what you contributed? "I wrote the opening, the conflict, the character's decision, and the ending. I used one AI suggestion for line 3 and rewrote it. Lines 6 and 9 are entirely mine." That's what AI-assisted sounds like when you know what you made.

✅ Caregiver check-in

  • What was your child's score on the ownership meter? Was it 7 or above?
  • Which lines were the hardest to write? Which came most naturally?
  • Did they use any of the AI options? Did they change them, or use them as-is?
  • Can they explain the difference between AI-assisted and AI-generated in their own words?
  • If a teacher asked "what did you contribute to this?" — could they answer clearly?
For caregivers

Why this module exists — the research

This module addresses the most significant open question in 10–12 digital literacy right now: how children should relate to generative AI tools as creative collaborators. The research grounding comes from two intersecting areas — creative identity development and media literacy.

Erik Erikson's stage of industry vs inferiority (ages 6–12) identifies children's core developmental drive as wanting to produce real, competent work. Children this age are motivated by the feeling of genuine accomplishment, not just completion. A module that frames AI as a tool for producing output faster misses this entirely — and likely backfires. The My Line / AI Line rule works precisely because it preserves the child's sense of genuine authorship while giving them meaningful exposure to how AI tools function.

The AI-assisted vs AI-generated distinction matters for an immediate practical reason: schools are developing AI use policies, and children who understand this distinction can navigate them honestly. It also matters for longer-term creative identity — children who understand what they actually contributed to a piece of creative work develop stronger creative self-concept than children who cannot identify what was theirs.

The age restriction note (13+ for most AI chat tools) is not optional framing — it is the correct legal and terms-of-service position. Children who use AI tools under a parent's account in a supervised session are operating within what the platforms intend for under-13 access.

The safety signal — AI writing tools do not ask personal questions about home or school — is directly relevant to online safety at this age. Children who understand that legitimate AI tools are text tools with no interest in personal details can recognise when a platform framed as "AI" is attempting something different.

How to approach this activity

✅ Helpful approaches

  • Let the child choose their prompt and run the activity independently — the kid-leads format is intentional at this age
  • Ask about their ownership score: "How many were yours?" Not "was the story good?"
  • If they used an AI option: "Did you change it? Does it sound like you?" — these are the useful questions
  • The no-login variant is the default and the best one for most settings — pre-printed options remove account and network friction entirely

🚫 Avoid

  • Letting the child create their own AI tool account — the 13+ minimum applies
  • Framing AI as "cheating" — the lesson is about understanding the tool, not avoiding it
  • Praising the story quality rather than the creative decisions — quality is not the point; authorship is
  • Skipping the ownership meter — marking where the story falls is the reflective step that makes the distinction concrete
For library programme facilitators and school staff:

This activity is fully self-contained without any device or internet access. Print the story scaffold and AI options for each participant. The three story prompts and pre-written AI sentence options are included in this page and can be printed directly — no additional preparation required beyond printing.

The no-login variant is the appropriate choice for library settings: it avoids account management, parental consent logistics, network restrictions, and age-verification requirements. Do not facilitate children signing into any AI tool account during a library session.

The ownership meter works well as a physical sticker activity in group settings — each child marks their score with a sticker on a printed 1–10 scale. The group debrief question: "Who got 7 or above? What made lines 7, 8, and 10 the hardest?" — this surfaces the actual creative decision-making and makes the distinction concrete.

If a child asks "can I use AI for my school assignment?" — the correct response is "check with your teacher first." School AI policies are evolving; this activity gives children the vocabulary to have that conversation accurately.
