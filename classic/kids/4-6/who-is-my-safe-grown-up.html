Who is my safe grown-up?

Help your child name the trusted adults in their life — and practise the most important safety phrase they'll ever learn.

←
Did the previous activity? Secret stuff and share stuff
These two activities go together — do them in the same session if you can.

💡 The big idea

A safe grown-up is an adult your child can go to any time they feel confused, scared, or unsure — about something on a screen, or anything else. Knowing who your safe grown-ups are before something feels wrong makes it much easier to ask for help when it matters.

⭐
Safe grown-ups never ask you to keep secrets
from other safe grown-ups.
This is the golden rule. If an adult — online or in person — asks a child to keep something secret from their parents or caregivers, that is a warning sign. Say this to your child plainly and often.
Connection to Secret stuff and share stuff:
Knowing SECRET STUFF from SHARE STUFF only works if your child knows exactly who to go to when they're not sure. These two skills build on each other. "When in doubt — go to your safe grown-up" is the bridge.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Who can be a safe grown-up?

Safe grown-ups are adults who your child knows and trusts. They do not have to be family members. Even 1 or 2 safe grown-ups is enough.

A teacher, a librarian, or a school counsellor counts — especially for children with smaller family networks. The list does not need to be long. It needs to be real.

🎮 The activity

This is a conversation activity, not a worksheet. You guide; your child responds out loud.

  1. Ask your child: "Who are your safe grown-ups? Name as many as you can think of." Write them down in the boxes below (or just say them out loud). Even one or two is a great start.
  2. For each person your child names, confirm: "Yes! If something feels wrong, you can always go to [name]. They will help you."
  3. Practise the phrase together. Point to yourself and say: "Try saying: 'I need to talk to a safe grown-up.'" Say it with them. Say it three times.
  4. Ask: "What if you felt scared by something on a screen? What would you do?" The goal: they say your name, or another safe grown-up's name.
  5. Remind them of the golden rule one more time: "Safe grown-ups never ask you to keep something secret from me. If that ever happens — that's when you need to come and tell me right away."

🗣️ The phrase to practise

"I need to talk to a safe grown-up."
Say this with your child three times. Then ask them to say it alone. It should feel easy and normal — not scary.
Why practise a phrase?
When children are scared or confused, they often freeze. Having a specific, practised phrase gives them something concrete to do — like a fire drill for safety situations. The phrase works for online situations, in-person situations, and anything in between.
🛡️

The Safety Champion Sticker

Give a sticker every time your child practises the phrase, names a safe grown-up unprompted, or comes to you with something that felt wrong or confusing. Each of those moments is exactly what this activity is building toward.

The sticker is for the courage to ask — not for perfect answers.

🌱 Signs it's working

  • Your child can name at least one safe grown-up without prompting.
  • They say "I'll ask my safe grown-up" when they encounter something confusing on a screen.
  • They use the phrase: "I need to talk to a safe grown-up."
  • They come to you proactively when something feels wrong — even something small.
Revisit this activity: As children grow and their social world expands, their list of safe grown-ups may change. Revisit this conversation at the start of each school year — a new teacher, a new counsellor, a new neighbour can all be added.
For caregivers

Why this skill matters — the research

Research on child safety consistently identifies one factor as the strongest predictor of a child disclosing abuse or unsafe situations: knowing who their safe adults are and having explicitly practised asking them for help. This is true for both in-person and online situations.

Finkelhor & Hashima (2001) found that children who could name a trusted adult were significantly more likely to disclose concerns. The disclosure gap is not a trust gap — it is a vocabulary and practise gap. Children who have not rehearsed asking for help are less likely to do so when it matters.

Naming 1 or 2 safe grown-ups is enough

Some children — particularly those in single-parent households, care arrangements, or with limited extended family — may struggle to name 3 or more safe adults. This is normal and does not reflect a problem. What matters is that the people named are real and reachable. A teacher, a school counsellor, or a librarian is a fully legitimate safe grown-up. Reinforce this explicitly: "Even one is enough. You have [name], and that's real."

For library programme facilitators and teachers:
Some children running this activity may be from foster care, domestic violence situations, recent bereavement, or other disrupted family circumstances. If a child struggles to name people or becomes upset during the naming activity, redirect gently: "Your teacher or your librarian can be a safe grown-up too. You don't have to list everyone right now." Do not press. The goal is one name, said with confidence — not a complete list.

The golden rule explained

"Safe grown-ups never ask you to keep secrets from other safe grown-ups" is gold-standard child protection language, widely used by child safety organisations, including the Canadian Centre for Child Protection's Commit to Kids program. It gives children a clear, testable rule that works in-person and online. If someone pressures a child to hide something from their parents — whether online or in person — that pressure itself is the warning sign. Children do not need to evaluate the content of the secret; they only need to notice the secrecy demand.

Good practice / avoid

✅ Good

  • Name specific people with your child — not just categories
  • Confirm: "Yes, [name] is a safe grown-up — you can always go to them"
  • Practise the phrase in a calm, normal moment (not after a scary event)
  • Revisit each school year as their world changes

🚫 Avoid

  • Pushing for a list of 3–5 names if 1–2 is what they have
  • Making the activity feel serious or scary — keep it warm and practical
  • Framing all strangers as dangerous — nuance matters at this age
  • Making them feel anxious about "what if" scenarios
