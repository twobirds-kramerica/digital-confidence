Secret stuff and share stuff

Help your child learn what to keep private, what's safe to share — and always ask a grown-up when they're not sure.

💡 The big idea

Some things belong to our family — they're our SECRET STUFF and we keep them close. Some things are totally fine to share with friends and new people — that's our SHARE STUFF. And when we're not sure? We ask a grown-up first.

🔒 SECRET STUFF Our family's private things. We keep these in the family — not for strangers or apps.
🤝 SHARE STUFF Safe to tell friends and people we meet. No problem sharing these!
🙋 ASK A GROWN-UP Not sure? Always check with a safe grown-up before sharing anything on a screen.
Why this matters on screens:
Apps, games, and websites sometimes ask for information they don't need. If your child knows SECRET STUFF from SHARE STUFF, they can pause and come get you instead of typing something private into a box.

🛒 What you'll need

  • 🟠 One orange or red card (SECRET STUFF)
  • 🟩 One green card (SHARE STUFF)
  • ⭐ Stickers for the "Privacy Protector" reward
  • 📋 This page — you read the statements aloud

No cards? No problem. Your child can point to one of your hands — left for SECRET, right for SHARE. Three hands up (both fists, or a shrug) for "ask a grown-up."

🎮 How to play

  1. Put the orange card and the green card in front of your child.
  2. Explain: "I'm going to say something. If it's SECRET STUFF — things we keep in the family — touch the orange card. If it's SHARE STUFF — totally safe to tell people — touch the green card. If you're not sure, give me a shrug. We'll figure it out together."
  3. Read each statement below, one at a time. Pause and let your child respond.
  4. Every time they correctly identify SECRET STUFF — give a sticker. Every honest shrug gets one too.
  5. For statement 5, pause and say: "That one's tricky — let's talk about it." Use the note below as a guide.

🗣️ The six statements

Read each one aloud. Answers and notes are for you — your child doesn't see them.

  • Statement 1
    "My favourite colour is [child's favourite]."
    🤝 SHARE STUFF A great opener — easy win to build confidence
  • Statement 2
    "Our home address is [your street]."
    🔒 SECRET STUFF If they don't know the address yet — that's fine. The skill is "don't share it," not "memorise it."
  • Statement 3
    "My first name is [child's first name]."
    🤝 SHARE STUFF First name alone, with a grown-up nearby, is safe to share
  • Statement 4
    "Our family's special code word is [word]."
    🔒 SECRET STUFF If you don't have a family code word, use: "Our WiFi password" or "The number of steps to our door"
  • Statement 5 — talk about this one
    "I'm home alone right now."
    🔒 SECRET STUFF Secret from strangers and apps — but always tell a safe grown-up
  • Statement 6 — safety tie-in
    "A game asked me to type my home address to get a free prize."
    🔒 SECRET STUFF Real prizes never need your address from a child
💬 After statement 5, say: "Home alone is a secret from strangers and from apps — but it's never a secret from our safe grown-ups. If you ever feel unsafe at home, you always tell me or [name another safe adult]." This prevents the lesson from teaching children to hide unsafe situations from all adults.
💬 After statement 6, say: "Real games never need your address. If anything on a screen asks for our address, our phone number, or where I work — that's SECRET STUFF. Close the app and come get me."
🔒

The Privacy Protector Sticker

Every time your child spots SECRET STUFF — or gives an honest shrug and asks you instead of guessing — give them a sticker. The sticker is for the pause and the question, not the certainty. At 10–15 stickers, trade for a shared activity they pick.

The goal is to make "I'm not sure, let me ask" feel like a win — because it is.

🌱 Signs it's working

  • Your child uses the words "secret stuff" or "share stuff" during the day without prompting.
  • They come to you before entering personal information into any app or website.
  • They say "that's secret stuff" when someone asks for something that feels private.
  • They understand that some things are secret from strangers — but never from safe grown-ups.

Why this skill matters — the research

Children aged 4–6 have not yet developed the abstract reasoning to distinguish between information that is "safe" to share and information that creates real-world risk. This activity introduces a concrete, binary vocabulary — SECRET / SHARE — that gives them a decision rule they can apply independently as they become more digitally active.

Research by Livingstone & Helsper (2010) found that children who have explicit household rules about what to share online report higher confidence in navigating requests for personal information. The key is making the rule concrete and practised — not abstract ("be careful") — before the child encounters the real situation.

About the "home alone" statement

Statement 5 draws an important distinction: being home alone is SECRET STUFF from strangers and from apps — but it is never secret from safe grown-ups. Children this age sometimes overgeneralise privacy rules. Reinforce explicitly: "Safe grown-ups always need to know where you are and if you feel unsafe. That's never a secret."

If your child does not yet know your home address, that's age-appropriate. The skill is not "memorise it" — it's "don't share it with strangers." Praise the protective instinct regardless.

A note on DCC and your child's privacy

DCC does not collect names, addresses, passwords, or any information in this "Secret Stuff" category. No registration is required. No data leaves this page. We built it this way on purpose — because a digital literacy programme that practises what it preaches is the only kind worth trusting.

Good practice / avoid

✅ Good

  • Use real examples from your family (your actual code word, a real address)
  • Praise the shrug — "asking first" is the safest answer
  • Pair with the "safe grown-up" activity immediately after
  • Revisit monthly — the world changes fast

🚫 Avoid

  • Framing secrets as bad — some secrets (surprises, gifts) are fine
  • Testing with scary online safety scenarios too early
  • Implying strangers are always dangerous — nuance matters
  • Shaming a wrong answer about what's private
