Blackmail Has a Playbook

Someone threatening to share images of a person — real or faked — isn’t improvising. They’re running a script that’s been run on thousands of people, and every step of it is known. This module names those steps, because a trick you can name has less power. If it’s happening to you right now, help is right below — not at the bottom of the page.

Scenario 1 of 4
1 The instant connection

New follower, moving very fast

A guy in your year gets a new follower: attractive profile, says they go to a school two towns over, into the same things he is. Within a day they’re chatting constantly. By day three the messages are flirty, they want to move the chat to a more private app, and they’re hinting about trading photos.

What’s the biggest tell that something’s off?

What’s actually going on

That exact sequence — instant connection, heavy flattery, “let’s move somewhere private,” then a push for photos — is the opening of a scheme called financial sextortion, and it’s mostly run at industrial scale by organized groups working through hundreds of fake accounts at once. The US national tipline logged over 50,000 reports of it in 2025 alone, up 37 per cent in a year. In Canada, about nine in ten victims are boys aged 15 to 17.

Not because boys that age are careless — because that’s who the script is aimed at. The flattery is copy-paste. The “connection” is a template. Falling for a script written by professionals and tested on thousands of people isn’t stupidity; it’s the script working as designed. Knowing the shape of the opening is most of the defence.

2 The threat

Your friend gets the message

A friend comes to you in a panic. The person they’d been chatting with has flipped: they say they have a private image and will send it to your friend’s followers, family, and school unless they pay $200 by tonight.

What actually works here?

What’s actually going on

Paying almost never ends it — it tells them the pressure works, and the demands usually continue or grow. Negotiating keeps the conversation alive, which is exactly what they want. And deleting everything destroys the evidence that gets accounts shut down. The playbook that works: stop responding, screenshot the threats and usernames, and report it to Cybertip.ca — Canada’s national tipline handles roughly ten of these reports every single day. They’ve seen this exact script, they know the next steps, and they can help get images taken down.

And the thing your friend most needs to hear, ideally from you: this is a crime being committed against them. Not a mistake they have to quietly pay for. The law, the tipline, and everyone worth listening to are on their side.

3 The silence lever

Why the threat always says “everyone will see”

The threats in these schemes name the same people every time: your parents. Your school. Your followers. Your team. “Imagine what they’ll think of you.” It’s written to make the situation feel unsurvivable.

Why do blackmailers work so hard on that feeling?

What’s actually going on

The whole scheme runs on one fuel: silence. The blackmailer’s only real power is the victim being too ashamed to bring anyone else in. The moment a trusted adult or Cybertip.ca is in the loop, they’re no longer dealing with one scared person — and running the scam at scale means most of them cut their losses and move on rather than follow through. The “this would destroy my life” feeling is manufactured on purpose. It’s the product they’re selling.

And even in the worst case — something does get shared — it is not the end of the world the messages make it feel like. There are takedown processes that work, and the people who matter side with the person who was targeted, not the criminal. If telling parents feels impossible as a first step, Kids Help Phone (1-800-668-6868, or text CONNECT to 686868) is a real, confidential person who can help figure out the next move — at any hour.

4 The fake image

“But I never sent anything”

A guy gets a message from a stranger claiming to have an explicit image of him. He’s never sent one to anyone, so he almost laughs — until the image arrives. It’s fake, made with AI, but it looks real enough to scare him.

Does the image being fake change what he should do?

What’s actually going on

AI image tools mean this scheme no longer needs a real photo — blackmailers can manufacture one and run the exact same script. Which tells you something important: the playbook never depended on the image. It depends on panic and silence. Same script, same counter-move: don’t engage, keep the evidence, report it. In Canada, making or sharing intimate images of anyone under 18 is a crime whether the image is real or AI-generated — the fake is the blackmailer’s crime, not your problem to disprove.

If anything, the fake is their weakness. The target knows it’s manufactured, which makes the “everyone will believe this” threat easier to see for what it is: scenario 3’s silence lever, wearing a new tool. For more on spotting AI-generated fakes in general, AI in Real Life covers the wider pattern.

First-try answers. The score was never the point — the point is that the script is now visible, and a script you can see coming has already lost most of its power.

The four things worth keeping

  • The opening is a script: instant connection + fast escalation + “let’s go somewhere private” is a pattern, not a person falling for you.
  • Never pay, never negotiate. Stop replying, keep the evidence, report to Cybertip.ca.
  • Shame is the engine. Bringing in one safe person — or Cybertip.ca — cuts the power to the whole scheme.
  • Fake image, same playbook, same counter-move. And it’s a crime against you either way — never your fault.

The help block, one more time
