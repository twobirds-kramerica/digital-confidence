Social Media and Your Teen: The Grounded Start

Not a lecture about screens rotting brains, and not a pep talk about digital natives. What Canadian research and guidance actually say about teens aged 13–17 online — the platforms, the hours, the real risks, the genuine upsides — so the conversations at your kitchen table start from facts. Finish the 25-question check at 85% and print your certificate.

  • Free, no account, no tracking

1. Start with the honest picture

Social media is not a corner of your teen’s life; it is one of the rooms their social life happens in. Statistics Canada’s Canadian Internet Use Survey found that in 2022, 99% of young Canadians aged 15 to 24 used the internet and 91% used social networking sites — and 42% of youth spent 20 or more hours a week online for general use, compared with 25% of the overall population.

That means “just stay off it” is roughly the same instruction as “just don’t go where your friends are.” It can be the right call for a particular kid at a particular time — but it is a big call, and this course treats it as one option among several, not the default.

Source: Statistics Canada, Canadian Internet Use Survey (2022) — reported in Online activities of Canadian youth, cybervictimization and exposure to harmful content.

2. What teens actually use — think functions, not apps

App names churn every couple of years; the functions barely move. If you learn the functions, you never fall behind:

  • Group messaging — the modern hallway conversation. Group chats are where most teen social life, and most teen social pressure, actually happens.
  • Short video feeds — algorithm-picked, autoplaying, engineered to make the next clip frictionless. This is where most of the “where did two hours go” time lives.
  • Disappearing or ephemeral posts — content that claims to vanish after viewing. The claim is design, not physics: screenshots and second-phone photos defeat it trivially.
  • Gaming with voice and chat — for many teens (boys especially), games are the social platform, complete with strangers, spending pressure, and chat.
  • Direct messages from strangers — nearly every platform has them, most default to open, and they are the entry point for the ugliest risks in the advanced course.

MediaSmarts — the Ottawa-based charity whose digital media literacy resources are used in classrooms across every province and territory, and whose Young Canadians in a Wireless World study has surveyed over 20,000 Canadian students, parents, and teachers since 2000 — consistently finds that what shapes young people’s online experience is less which app they use than whether the adults around them are engaged with how they use it.

Source: MediaSmarts, Young Canadians in a Wireless World research series.

3. Screen time: what Canadian guidance actually says

You may be braced for “two hours maximum, full stop.” That is not what the Canadian Paediatric Society says about teenagers. Its position statement Digital media: Promoting healthy screen use in school-aged children and adolescents (2019, reaffirmed 2025) deliberately avoids a universal hour cap for adolescents, because the evidence is more interesting than a number: both zero use and excessive use are associated with worse outcomes, while moderate use — typically reported as around two to four hours a day — is associated with some cognitive and psychosocial benefits.

Instead of a stopwatch, the CPS gives parents four verbs — the “four Ms”:

  • Manage — make a family media plan together; use settings and controls deliberately, not as an ambush.
  • Meaningful — care about what the screen time is. An hour building something or talking to real friends is not the same hour as passive autoplay.
  • Model — your own phone habits are the loudest lesson in the house. Teens clock the double standard instantly.
  • Monitor — watch for the real warning signs: screen use that interferes with sleep, school, activity, or face-to-face life.

The pattern matters over time, too. A Statistics Canada longitudinal study following the same young people from 2019 (ages 12–17) to 2023 found that 58% of youth who met screen time guidelines at both points reported excellent or very good mental health, compared with 38% of those who exceeded the guidelines at both points. That is an association, not a verdict on any one household — but it is a good reason to care about the pattern rather than policing every Tuesday.

Sources: Canadian Paediatric Society, Digital media: Promoting healthy screen use in school-aged children and adolescents (2019, reaffirmed 2025); Statistics Canada, Does screen time make a difference? A longitudinal study of youth screen time and well-being (Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth, 2019 and 2023).

4. The upsides are real — say so out loud

A parent who only ever names the dangers loses credibility with a teen who experiences the benefits daily. The benefits are legitimate:

  • Connection — friendships maintained across schools, moves, and shift-work family schedules; for many teens the group chat is where belonging lives.
  • Identity and community — a teen who feels like the only one of their kind in their school — by interest, culture, orientation, or circumstance — can find their people. The CPS notes digital media can support identity development and social connection in adolescence.
  • Learning and creating — tutorials, languages, coding, music production, editing. A great deal of genuine skill-building starts on the same platforms that host the junk.
  • Help-seeking — many young people reach out for mental-health support online before they will say a word in person. Kids Help Phone exists as a texting service partly for exactly this reason.

Naming the upsides is not going soft. It is the price of admission to the risk conversation — a teen listens differently to an adult who clearly sees the whole picture.

Source: Canadian Paediatric Society position statement, benefits of digital media in adolescence.

5. The risks, sized honestly

The introductory risk map, with Canadian numbers where they exist:

  • Harmful content finds them — they do not have to go looking. Statistics Canada found 74% of youth reported seeing online content that may incite hate or violence at least once in the past year (versus 51% of the overall population), and 34% reported seeing intimate images shared without the person’s consent (versus 20%).
  • Cyberbullying — the group pile-on, the exclusion screenshot, the account made about someone. It follows kids home in a way schoolyard versions never did. The teen introductory course covers what teens themselves can do; your job at this level is being tellable (more below).
  • Mood effects — in Statistics Canada’s survey on social media in Canadians’ lives, about one in five users under 30 reported feeling anxious or depressed as a result of their social media use. Not everyone, not inevitable — but common enough to ask about.
  • Contact from strangers — open DMs mean adults and scammers can reach teens directly. This is the doorway to the serious harms (sextortion, grooming) covered in the advanced course — introduced here only so you know the door exists.
  • Spending pressure — loot boxes, in-game currency, limited-time shops. Designed pressure, aimed at kids. (The youth module Gems Are Real Money unpacks it — worth reading yourself.)

Sources: Statistics Canada, Young people and exposure to harmful online content, 2022; Statistics Canada, Canadians’ assessments of social media in their lives.

6. Parental controls: education, not surveillance

Controls are a legitimate tool — the CPS’s “Manage” explicitly includes them. They work best when they are announced, explained, and age-adjusted, and worst when they are secret, punitive, and permanent. The layers, from broadest to narrowest:

  • Device level — the built-in family tools on phones and tablets (screen-time schedules, app limits, content filters, purchase approval). The most practical layer for most families, and free.
  • App level — most major platforms now have supervision or “family pairing” modes: time limits, DM restrictions, content filtering. Get Cyber Safe — the Government of Canada’s national cyber-safety campaign — maintains plain-language guidance on securing devices and accounts.
  • Network level — filtering on your home wifi router. Broadest brush, and the easiest to walk around: it stops at your front door, which matters in the advanced course.
  • Account level — correct birthdates on accounts, so the platform’s own under-18 protections actually apply; privacy settings reviewed together, not confiscation-style.

Two rules of thumb that keep controls educational rather than adversarial: do it with them, not to them (a control your teen helped configure teaches judgment; a secret one teaches evasion), and loosen deliberately as they age — a 16-year-old under 13-year-old settings has every incentive to route around all of it.

Sources: Canadian Paediatric Society (family media plans, parental controls under “Manage”); Get Cyber Safe, Government of Canada.

7. The one thing that outperforms every setting: being tellable

Every serious online harm has the same choke point: whether the teen tells an adult early or hides it until it is a crisis. Kids who expect blame and confiscation hide things; kids who expect help tell sooner. That single variable — tellability — does more work than any filter you will ever configure.

Three habits that build it:

  • Ask about the good stuff too. If every conversation about their phone is an inspection, you have taught them what conversations with you are for.
  • Pre-commit, out loud: “If something ever goes wrong online, come to me. Whatever it is, the phone is not the first thing that gets taken.” Then keep the promise.
  • React to the first small thing well. The first minor thing they bring you is a test. Handle it calmly and they will bring you the big one.

Numbers worth having before you need them

  • Kids Help Phone — for your teen: call 1-800-668-6868 or text CONNECT to 686868. Free, 24/7, confidential, anywhere in Canada. Worth putting in their phone together, framed as “for you or a friend.”
  • 9-8-8 — Canada’s Suicide Crisis Helpline: call or text 9-8-8, 24/7. For anyone, any age — including you. (If you have heard “811” — that is provincial health advice, not a crisis line.)
  • Cybertip.ca — if anything involving your child and intimate images or online sexual exploitation ever comes up: Cybertip.ca, Canada’s national tipline. The advanced course covers when and how.

The whole course in five lines

  • Social platforms are where teen social life partly lives (StatCan: 91% of 15–24s) — treat bans as a big call, not a default.
  • Canadian paediatric guidance says pattern and content over stopwatch: the four Ms, with moderate use (~2–4 h/day) linked to some benefits.
  • Name the upsides out loud — it is the price of credibility on the risks.
  • Controls are education when done with your teen and loosened with age; they are evasion training when secret and punitive.
  • Being tellable beats every filter. Pre-commit: telling you ≠ losing the phone.
