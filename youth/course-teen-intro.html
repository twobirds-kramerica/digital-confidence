Your Feed, Your Call

Nobody here is going to tell you to quit social media. This is the other thing: what the apps actually give you, what they quietly take, what Canadian research really says about screen time (spoiler: there is no magic hour number), and the two or three facts that are worth knowing before you need them. 1. The part adults skip: what you actually get out of it

Let’s start where most talks never do. Statistics Canada found 91% of young Canadians (15–24) use social networking — because it works. It keeps friendships alive across schools and moves. It finds you your people when your school doesn’t have them. It teaches things — editing, music, languages, code — faster than most classrooms. And for a lot of people your age, it’s the first place they ever ask for help, because typing something is easier than saying it.

All of that is real, and you don’t have to defend it. This course exists because the same apps also have machinery running under the surface — and knowing how the machinery works is the difference between using the app and being used by it.

Source: Statistics Canada, Online activities of Canadian youth (Canadian Internet Use Survey, 2022).

2. The machinery: why the feed never runs out

Quick version (the modules Who’s Paying for Your Attention? and Who Picked This For You? go deep): the apps are free because your attention is the product being sold to advertisers. Every design choice — infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks, notification timing — is tuned to one metric: keep you there longer. That’s not a conspiracy theory; it’s the business model, and the people who built it say so openly.

Why it matters here: when you’ve been scrolling for two hours and feel like you have no self-control — that’s not a character flaw. You’re in a tug-of-war with a system engineered by professionals to win it. Naming that changes the question from “what’s wrong with me?” to “how do I want to play this?” — which is a question you can actually do something with.

3. Screen time: what the Canadian evidence actually says

You’ve probably heard “two hours max” like it’s carved in stone. Here’s the actual state of Canadian guidance: the Canadian Paediatric Society — the national association of children’s doctors — deliberately does not set a universal hour cap for teenagers. Their reading of the evidence: zero screen use and excessive screen use are both linked with worse outcomes, while moderate use — roughly two to four hours a day — is associated with some genuine cognitive and social benefits. What matters more than the stopwatch is what the time is spent on, and whether it’s crowding out sleep, school, movement, or in-person time.

The long game shows up in the data too. Statistics Canada followed the same young Canadians from 2019 to 2023: among those whose screen time stayed within guidelines at both points, 58% reported excellent or very good mental health — versus 38% of those who exceeded guidelines both times. Association, not destiny. But it’s a reason the pattern is worth caring about, even when any single Tuesday isn’t.

The most useful self-test isn’t a number at all — it’s the one from How Do You Feel After?: after an hour on the app, do you feel better, or worse? Your own answer, honestly noticed, beats any rule anyone hands you.

Sources: Canadian Paediatric Society, Digital media: Promoting healthy screen use in school-aged children and adolescents (2019, reaffirmed 2025); Statistics Canada, longitudinal study of youth screen time and well-being (2019 and 2023).

4. The stuff that finds you without being searched for

Two Statistics Canada numbers worth knowing, because they’re about your age group specifically. In one year: 74% of Canadian youth saw online content that could incite hate or violence (versus 51% of everyone). And 34% saw intimate images that may have been shared without the person’s consent (versus 20% of everyone). Feeds push extreme content because extreme content holds attention — see the machinery section.

Also from the national data: about one in five social media users under 30 said the apps have made them feel anxious or depressed. If that’s ever you, you’re not broken and you’re not alone — it’s common enough that Statistics Canada measures it. It’s also fixable: feeds can be retrained, accounts muted, apps deleted and reinstalled later. You have more control than the design wants you to notice.

Sources: Statistics Canada, Young people and exposure to harmful online content, 2022; Statistics Canada, Canadians’ assessments of social media in their lives.

5. Bullying: the online version has different physics

Offline, a bad moment ends when you walk away. Online it has three properties that make it heavier: it follows you home (the group chat is in your pocket at 11 p.m.), it scales (a pile-on can go from three people to three hundred in an hour), and it persists (screenshots outlive deleted posts). Knowing the physics tells you the counter-moves:

  • Don’t perform for the pile-on. Replying angry feeds it content. The strongest move usually looks like the weakest: don’t engage.
  • Screenshot first, then block and report. Evidence beats memory. Every platform has reporting tools; they work more often than people think, especially with evidence attached.
  • If you’re a witness, you’re a participant — pick which kind. A pile-on runs on the silence of everyone watching. One private “that was messed up, you okay?” to the target does more than you’d guess.
  • Tell someone when it’s heavy. Not because you can’t handle it — because nobody should have to solo-carry three hundred people’s worth of garbage. A parent, a teacher you trust, or Kids Help Phone (below) all count.

6. The screenshot rule: the one fact that changes decisions

Everything you send can be captured by the person you send it to. “Disappearing” messages disappear from the interface — not from a screenshot, not from a second phone pointed at the screen. This isn’t a reason to never post anything; it’s a reason to run one quick check before sending the risky thing: would this be okay if it ended up in front of the person I’d least want to see it? Your future self is the person you’re protecting, and future-you is worth it.

That 34% number from section 4 — a third of Canadian youth have seen intimate images shared without consent — is what happens when people trust the interface instead of the physics. The advanced course, The Stuff Nobody Puts in the Group Chat, covers that whole territory properly: the law (which is more on your side than you think), what to do if an image is out there, and why none of it is ever the target’s fault.

Source: Statistics Canada, Young people and exposure to harmful online content, 2022.

If something online is getting heavy

None of these require it to be an emergency. “I just need to talk to someone” is a fully valid reason.

  • Kids Help Phone — call 1-800-668-6868 or text CONNECT to 686868. Free, 24/7, confidential, anywhere in Canada. A real person, including at 3 a.m.
  • 9-8-8 — if things ever feel darker than “heavy”: call or text 9-8-8, Canada’s Suicide Crisis Helpline. 24/7, any age.
  • Cybertip.ca — if anyone has, or claims to have, intimate images of you or someone you know: Cybertip.ca. Not your fault, fixable, and they handle this exact thing every day.

The whole course in five lines

  • The upsides are real — connection, community, learning, help — and you don’t have to defend them.
  • The app is free because your attention is the product. Losing hours isn’t weak willpower; it’s engineered.
  • No magic hour cap: Canadian paediatric guidance says ~2–4 balanced hours beats both zero and endless. “How do I feel after?” is the honest test.
  • Bullying online follows, scales, and persists — so: don’t feed it, screenshot it, report it, and never solo-carry it.
  • Screenshots beat “disappearing.” One pre-send check protects future-you.
