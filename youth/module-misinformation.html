Is This Actually True?

Some of what crosses your feed today will be false — not because you follow the wrong people, but because false stuff is engineered to travel. This module isn’t “trust nothing” and it isn’t a lecture about the news. It’s four situations, the machinery behind each one, and a check that takes about thirty seconds. Nobody’s grading your politics.

  • Four situations
  • You choose, then we debrief
  • Zero “kids these days” lectures
Scenario 1 of 4
1 The screenshot

“BREAKING” in the group chat

A screenshot lands in the group chat: a headline in news-site font saying a celebrity died, with “BREAKING” in red. No link — just the image. Two people have already replied “WHAT” and one has reposted it to their story. It looks exactly like a real news site.

What does the screenshot actually prove?

What’s actually going on

A screenshot is just pixels. Free tools can generate a fake headline in any outlet’s exact style in under a minute — and a screenshot conveniently deletes the one thing that would let you check it: the link. That’s not always malicious (people screenshot real news too, which is why answer D is also wrong), but it means the image itself proves nothing either way.

MediaSmarts — Canada’s digital media literacy organization — built its Break the Fake program around four quick checks: use a fact-checking tool, find the original source, verify that source, and see if other outlets are reporting it. Each one usually takes under a minute, and most of the time one is enough. Here, step two settles it: search the headline. If a real outlet published it, it’s on their site and every other news site within minutes. If the only place it exists is a screenshot — you have your answer.

2 The video that looks real

“I checked the hands and everything”

A clip is going around of a famous athlete announcing something shocking. A friend says they verified it: “I know how to spot AI. I checked the hands, the blinking, the lighting. It’s real.” The video does look flawless — voice, face, everything.

What’s wrong with “I checked the hands”?

What’s actually going on

The weird-hands era is ending. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security — the federal government’s cyber defence agency — warns in its public guidance on false information (ITSAP.00.300) that generative AI is now used to produce deepfakes and spread false and misleading content at scale. Every “how to spot a deepfake” trick has an expiry date, because the next generation of tools trains on exactly those flaws. If your whole method is looking harder at the video, you’re in an arms race your eyes will eventually lose.

The move that doesn’t expire is to stop looking at the clip and look around it instead — open a new tab and search what independent sources say. Fact-checkers call this lateral reading, and it’s what CTRL-F, a Canadian program from the civic-education charity CIVIX used in schools across the country, teaches as the core skill. A real announcement from a famous athlete leaves a paper trail everywhere within the hour: their own accounts, their team, actual news outlets. A deepfake has exactly one source — the account that posted it.

3 Built to enrage

The post that makes your blood boil

A post claims a group of students did something outrageous at a school across the country. It’s infuriating. The comments are a war zone, the shares are climbing, and your thumb is already on the repost button — because people need to know about this.

The fury you’re feeling right now — what is it, most likely?

What’s actually going on

False content doesn’t spread because people are stupid. It spreads because it’s optimized — for exactly the jolt you just felt. The Cyber Centre’s guidance describes how false information campaigns deliberately play on emotions to get amplified, and the previous module in this series showed why: the feed promotes whatever gets engagement, and nothing engages like outrage. A false story doesn’t have to survive fact-checking. It only has to survive the two seconds between your eyes and your thumb.

So flip the signal: a strong emotional spike is your cue to check, not to share. Not because anger is wrong — sometimes the story is true and the anger is earned — but because “this makes me furious” is precisely the reaction the fakes are built to trigger. One more thing worth knowing: reposting false stuff, even sincerely, is how most of it travels. The people spreading misinformation are mostly not liars. They’re people who were sure.

4 The thirty-second check

“Nobody has time to fact-check everything”

A friend hears all this and shrugs: “Be real. Nobody’s going to research every post like a homework assignment. Either you trust your feed or you log off forever.”

Are those really the only two options?

What’s actually going on

The homework framing is the trap. You don’t have to verify your whole feed — scrolling past something unverified costs nothing. The checkpoint is the share button: the moment something is about to travel through you is the moment it deserves thirty seconds. And thirty seconds is genuinely what it takes. The toolkit, all free: new tab, search the claim and see if anyone independent confirms it (the lateral-reading move from scenario 2); reverse image search a suspicious photo to see where it really came from — often a real image from a different year and country wearing a new caption; and check a fact-checker, since viral fakes usually get debunked within hours. Those are three of MediaSmarts’ four Break the Fake steps, and their point is that you rarely need more than one.

Waiting for platforms to label everything false doesn’t work — labels lag, and plenty of false content never gets one. The realistic version of this skill isn’t “trust nothing.” It’s share slower than you scroll. That single habit removes you from the delivery chain that misinformation depends on.

First-try answers. The score was never the point — the point is that “it looks real” stopped being evidence, and you now have a check that costs thirty seconds.

The four things worth keeping

  • A screenshot with no link proves nothing. Search the headline — real news exists in more than one place.
  • “It looks real” is not verification anymore. Good fakes pass the eye test; check around the clip, not inside it.
  • A strong emotional spike is the cue to check, not to share. Outrage is the delivery mechanism.
  • You don’t have to verify your feed — just what you share. Thirty seconds: search the claim, reverse-search the image, check a fact-checker.
