The Stuff Nobody Puts in the Group Chat

The harder half: images that don’t come back, what Canadian law actually says about intimate photos (it’s more on your side than you think), deepfakes, blackmail scripts, and the hidden-phone life. Zero blame anywhere on this page — just the facts that are worth having before you need them, and exactly what to do if you need them now. 85% on the 25-question check earns the certificate.

If something is happening right now

You don’t have to read this page first. And whatever has already happened: it is not your fault, and it is fixable.

  • Someone has, or claims to have, intimate images of you: report it at Cybertip.ca — Canada’s national tipline deals with this about ten times a day. NeedHelpNow.ca walks you through getting images taken down, step by step.
  • You need a person, right now, confidentially: Kids Help Phone — call 1-800-668-6868 or text CONNECT to 686868. Free, 24/7, a real human, including at 3 a.m.
  • If things feel darker than heavy: call or text 9-8-8 — Canada’s Suicide Crisis Helpline, 24/7.

1. Permanence, without the lecture

You know the theory: the internet is forever. Here’s the practical version. Anything you send exists on the recipient’s device the moment it arrives. “Disappearing” deletes it from the app’s interface — not from a screenshot, not from another phone pointed at the screen, not from a recording. From that moment, what happens to it is governed by one thing only: the judgment of the person holding it. Today’s trusted person and next year’s ex are sometimes the same person.

Scale check, from Statistics Canada: 34% of Canadian youth reported seeing intimate images that may have been shared without the person’s consent, in a single year. A third. This isn’t a rare lightning strike; it’s a common failure with a known cause — trusting the interface instead of the physics.

None of this means “never trust anyone.” It means the decision to send an intimate image is bigger than the app makes it feel, and it deserves about sixty more seconds of thought than the chat’s pace wants to give you. That’s it. That’s the lecture.

Source: Statistics Canada, Young people and exposure to harmful online content, 2022.

2. The law: more on your side than you think

Most teens learn the law here from rumours, and the rumours run backwards — they make targets feel like criminals and let the people doing harm feel safe. The actual Canadian position:

  • Sharing an intimate image of anyone without their consent is a crime. Criminal Code s.162.1, since 2015. If someone shares an image of you without consent, a crime was committed against you. You are the victim, not the accused.
  • Images of anyone under 18 are in a stricter category entirely. Canadian law treats intimate images of minors as child sexual abuse material: making, possessing, or forwarding them is a serious crime — including when teens forward images of other teens. The classmate who forwards an image “as a joke” is the one committing the offence.
  • “But they sent it to me willingly” is not a licence. Whatever the story of the original photo, forwarding it to even one other person is where the serious criminality lands. If you’re ever holding an image like that: delete it, don’t forward it, and don’t be the link in the chain.
  • The takeaway if you’re a target: the fear that reporting gets you in trouble is exactly the fear that people who share images count on. Cybertip.ca exists for you, not against you.

Sources: Criminal Code of Canada, s.162.1 (non-consensual distribution of intimate images, in force 2015); Cybertip.ca / Canadian Centre for Child Protection; MediaSmarts, sexting and the law.

3. If an image is already out there

The playbook, in order. It works whether the image is real, edited, or fully fake:

  • It is not your fault. Not a formality — the whole harm model of image-sharing runs on the target feeling too ashamed to act. Refusing the shame is move one.
  • Evidence before cleanup: screenshot where it’s posted, usernames, URLs. Then report.
  • NeedHelpNow.ca — built by the Canadian Centre for Child Protection for exactly this: step-by-step help getting images off platforms. Takedown works far more often than the panic says it will.
  • Cybertip.ca — the national report. This activates the machinery that gets accounts shut down.
  • One adult in the loop. Pick the calmest one you have — parent, aunt, coach, school counsellor. If that feels impossible today, Kids Help Phone (1-800-668-6868, text CONNECT to 686868) is a confidential first step that can help you plan the second one.

4. Wait — blackmail has a playbook too

If anyone ever threatens to share images of you — images that exist, or images they claim exist, or images they faked — that’s sextortion, it runs on a known script, and this site has a full module on it: Blackmail Has a Playbook. The sixty-second version: never pay, never negotiate, stop replying, keep the evidence, report to Cybertip.ca. Cybertip handles about ten of these a day; in Canada roughly nine in ten victims are boys 15–17, because that’s who the industrial-scale scripts target. Shame is the scheme’s only real engine — one safe person in the loop cuts its power.

Source: Cybertip.ca / Canadian Centre for Child Protection. Full module: Blackmail Has a Playbook.

5. Deepfakes: when the image was never real

AI tools can fabricate a convincing intimate image of you from an ordinary photo — a tagged team picture is enough. So someone who never sent anything to anyone can still get the “I have images of you” message, followed by a fake that looks real enough to cause panic. Three facts to hold onto:

  • The fake is their crime. In Canada, a fabricated intimate image of a person under 18 is treated the same as a real one — making it and sharing it are serious offences. You have nothing to disprove.
  • Same playbook as section 3: don’t engage, keep evidence, Cybertip.ca, NeedHelpNow.ca. The playbook never depended on the image being real, because the scheme never did either — it depends on panic and silence.
  • If it’s done to someone else — someone in your school makes a fake of a classmate — that’s not a prank with a tech twist; it’s the same criminal category as sharing a real image. Don’t share it, don’t rate it, report it. The target will remember who treated it as funny and who didn’t.

Sources: Cybertip.ca; see also AI in Real Life for spotting fakes generally.

6. The hidden phone: an honest cost-benefit

Your parents said no social media until sixteen. A friend has an old phone in a drawer. It has no SIM, but wifi is everywhere — the neighbour’s, the library’s, a hotspot at lunch. Twenty minutes later you have every banned app and an account that says you’re nineteen, because that’s what got past the sign-up screen.

No lecture — an honest accounting, because you’ve maybe already done the first half of the math (the freedom) and nobody showed you the second half (the fine print):

  • The nineteen-year-old account has adult settings. Every platform gives under-18 accounts extra protection: stranger DMs restricted, harsher content filtered, privacy defaults up. Your workaround account gets none of that. The sextortion scripts from section 4? Open stranger DMs are their front door, and you just propped it open.
  • The secret is the vulnerability. Run the tape: something goes wrong on the hidden phone — a threat, a leaked image, a chat that turned dark. Getting help now requires confessing the device, the account, and months of cover stories, all at the worst possible moment. That maths is exactly what blackmailers count on: a target who can’t tell anyone without blowing up their own secret is the perfect target.
  • The play with better odds: negotiate for real access — a set-up-together account with actual protections beats a secret one with none, even if it comes with conditions you’d rather skip. Most parents will trade supervised access for honesty; almost none will offer it while the drawer-phone is still secret. If the renegotiation genuinely can’t happen at your house, at minimum: put your real age on the account. The protections are for you, not for them.

7. When the pile-on is aimed at someone else

Advanced bullying isn’t about you being the target — it’s about the moment you’re standing in the audience. A pile-on, a leaked image being passed around, a fake being rated in a group chat: these run on the maths of the crowd. A hundred people watching silently reads, to the target, as a hundred people agreeing.

  • Don’t amplify: not sharing, not reacting with the laughing emoji, not “just asking” who it is. Every interaction is fuel.
  • One private message to the target — “that’s messed up, I’m not part of it, you okay?” — reliably lands as the loudest thing anyone did that day.
  • Report it — platform tools, and for anything involving intimate images of a minor, Cybertip.ca. Reporting isn’t snitching; forwarding an image of a minor is the crime (section 2), and reporting is the thing that stops it travelling further.
  • If carrying what you’ve seen gets heavy — witnesses get to use Kids Help Phone too.

The whole course in five lines

  • Sent = permanent. “Disappearing” is an interface promise; screenshots are physics. The decision deserves sixty more seconds than the chat gives it.
  • The law protects targets: non-consensual sharing is a crime against you (s.162.1), and forwarding images of anyone under 18 is a serious crime — even “as a joke,” even between teens.
  • Image out there? Not your fault → evidence → NeedHelpNow.ca → Cybertip.ca → one safe person.
  • Fakes change nothing: same playbook, same crime (theirs), nothing for you to disprove.
  • The hidden phone trades away your protections and makes you un-helpable at the worst moment. Negotiated access with real settings is the stronger play.
