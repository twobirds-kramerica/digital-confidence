How Do You Feel After?

You meant to check one thing. Forty-five minutes later you’re still scrolling and you feel worse, and you’re not sure why. That’s not a willpower problem — the app is built to do exactly that. This is about seeing the machine, and one honest question that beats counting minutes.

  • Four situations
  • You choose, then we debrief
  • Nobody’s telling you to quit anything
Scenario 1 of 4
1 The 45-minute hole

“I only opened it to check one thing”

You picked up your phone to see one message. Somehow you’re now deep in a stranger’s argument in the comments, then a disaster clip, then another. You look up and it’s been 45 minutes. You feel kind of tense and flat, and nothing actually happened.

What’s the most useful thing to notice here?

What’s actually going on

The feed never ends on purpose — there’s no “last page,” so there’s no natural stop. Counting your minutes afterward mostly just adds guilt, and guilt doesn’t change anything. The far more useful read is how the scroll left you: lighter or heavier, calmer or more wound up. Forty minutes that leave you laughing with friends is time well spent. Five minutes that leave you tense is the feed taking more than it gave.

This isn’t a lecture about being on your phone too much. It’s one swap: stop asking “how long was I on” and start asking “how do I feel now.”

2 Why is it all bad news?

The feed is basically rage and disaster now

You notice your For You page has turned into a wall of furious takes, screaming arguments, and “the world is ending” clips. You didn’t ask for that. It kind of stresses you out, but you keep watching.

Why does the upsetting stuff keep rising to the top?

What’s actually going on

Anger and fear travel faster than calm. A post that makes people furious gets comments, quote-tweets, stitches, shares — and the algorithm reads all that activity as “engaging” and pushes it to more people. Calm, ordinary posts quietly sink. So the feed drifts toward whatever winds you up, because winding you up keeps you scrolling. It’s not a picture of the world; it’s a picture of what keeps you here.

Two moves that actually work: hit “not interested” / mute / unfollow on the rage-bait (the account isn’t told), and deliberately linger on stuff you like so the algorithm feeds you more of that instead.

3 “You get two hours”

Is the two-hour limit an actual rule?

Someone tells you there’s an official Canadian rule that teens are “allowed two hours of screen time a day” and you’re breaking it. It doesn’t sound right, but you’re not sure.

What’s the honest answer?

What’s actually going on

The “2 hours” number comes from the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, and it’s about recreational screen time as one part of a balanced day — it’s a guideline, not a hard cap, and not a law. The Canadian Paediatric Society, which advises doctors here, actually moved away from a strict stopwatch for ages 5–17 toward “quality over quantity”: two hours making stuff or talking to friends is completely different from two hours doomscrolling.

Translation: nobody credible is handing you a magic number. A caring adult setting a limit is fair, but the honest measure isn’t the clock — it’s whether the time left you better or worse.

4 Running your own check

So what do you actually do with this?

You’ve clocked it: some apps leave you buzzing and connected, one specific app almost always leaves you flat and comparing yourself to everyone. You don’t want to nuke your whole phone.

What’s the actual move?

What’s actually going on

Doctors literally build this into their advice. The Canadian Paediatric Society’s checklist for healthy screen use has four parts — Manage, Meaningful, Model, Monitor — and “Monitor” is spelled out as watching for negative emotions after being online. That’s the same signal this whole module is about, straight from the guideline. You can run your own version.

It’s not quit-or-scroll-forever. It’s: notice how each app leaves you, then aim more time at the ones that leave you okay, mute the accounts that don’t, and open the drainy one on purpose when you actually want it — not just because your thumb went there.

First-try answers. The point was never the score — it’s that you can now read the feed instead of the feed reading you.

The four things worth keeping

  • The feed has no bottom on purpose. There’s no natural stop, so you make your own.
  • The upsetting stuff rises because anger and fear get reactions — the outrage loop, not the real world.
  • There’s no hard two-hour law for teens in Canada. Quality beats the stopwatch.
  • The honest signal is how you feel after. Even doctors measure it that way.
