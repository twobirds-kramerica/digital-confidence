Why the Feed Keeps You Scrolling

⏱ About 25 minutes, at your own pace

✅ You are in a safe place. Nothing on this page can harm your device.

Feeds are designed by very clever people to keep you scrolling. Knowing how they do it, and learning to notice how you feel after, puts you back in charge of your own time and mood.

Brenda's story

Brenda, 72, a retired teacher in southwestern Ontario, opened Facebook after lunch to see one photo her daughter had posted. Forty-five minutes later she was still scrolling, and she did not quite know how she had got there. One post about a local road closure had led to an angry argument in the comments, then a news clip that made her worried, then another, then another.

When she finally put the tablet down, she felt tired and a little anxious, though nothing bad had actually happened to her. She had not been careless. She had not done anything wrong. She had simply used an app that was built, very deliberately, to keep her there as long as possible.

Brenda did not need to delete Facebook. She loved seeing her grandchildren. What she needed was to understand how the feed works, and one small habit to stay in charge of it. That is exactly what this lesson gives you.

💬
From your coach

Let me say this clearly, because it matters: if a feed has ever swallowed an hour of your afternoon, that is not a lack of willpower on your part. These apps are designed by teams of engineers whose whole job is to hold your attention. You are not weak, and you are not behind. Once you can see how the feed works, it loses most of its grip. Nothing on this page can harm your device, and there is nothing here you can get wrong.

What a feed is, and why it never ends

A feed is the scrolling list of posts you see when you open Facebook, Instagram, a news app, or YouTube. You flick your finger upward and new posts appear, one after another, with no bottom to the page. This is called infinite scroll, and it is a deliberate design choice.

A printed newspaper ends. You reach the back page, you fold it up, and you are done. A feed is designed so that ending never comes. There is always one more post loading just below the one you are reading, so there is never a natural moment to stop.

Why there is no "bottom of the page"

Older websites showed you a page of results and then a button that said "Next page." That button was a natural pause: a moment where many people simply stopped. Feeds removed the button on purpose. When the next post loads automatically, your mind never gets the little nudge that says "you have finished." The scrolling continues because nothing tells it to stop.

Confidence check

You are not imagining it. The reason the feed feels endless is that it was built to be endless. Knowing this is the first step to deciding for yourself when you are done.

The slot-machine feeling

Have you noticed that scrolling can feel a bit like waiting to see what comes next? Most posts are ordinary. Then, every so often, something delightful appears: a photo of your grandchild, a funny video, a message from an old friend. You never know when that good moment is coming, so you keep flicking to find out.

This is the same design that makes slot machines in a casino so hard to walk away from. The reward is unpredictable. Because you cannot guess when the next good post will appear, your mind keeps you reaching for it, one more scroll, then one more. Designers call this a variable reward, and it is one of the most powerful tools they have.

This is not an accident

The unpredictable timing is not a happy coincidence. It is engineered. The app mixes the occasional wonderful post among many ordinary ones precisely because that keeps you scrolling longer than a steady, predictable stream ever would. Understanding this takes away a lot of its pull.

Confidence check

That "just one more" feeling is the design working exactly as intended. It says nothing about you. Once you can name it, you can decide whether to keep going or set the device down.

Why the upsetting posts rise to the top

You may have noticed that feeds often show you things that make you angry, worried, or outraged: a furious argument, an alarming headline, a story designed to frighten. This is not because the world is only bad news. It is because posts that stir up strong feelings get more reactions, and the app shows you more of whatever gets reactions.

When a post makes people angry, they comment, they argue, they share it. The app watches all of that activity and decides the post is "engaging," so it shows it to even more people. Calm, kind, ordinary posts get quietly pushed down. Over time, the feed fills up with the things most likely to upset you, because upset keeps you scrolling and commenting.

The word for it: the outrage loop

Experts call this the outrage loop or a "doom feed." Anger and fear travel faster online than calm and kindness, so the app learns to serve you more of what alarms you. It is not trying to inform you or to make you feel good. It is trying to keep you there, and strong emotion is the tool it uses.

You are allowed to scroll past

You never have to read an upsetting post, watch an alarming video, or join an angry argument in the comments. Scrolling straight past something, or closing the app entirely, is always a reasonable choice. You are not being rude, and you are not missing anything you are obliged to see.

Confidence check

If your feed leaves you feeling angry or frightened more often than not, that is the outrage loop at work, not a true picture of the world. You are allowed to look away.

The one question that changes everything

Here is the most useful idea in this whole lesson. Most advice about screens tells you to watch the clock: to count your minutes and feel guilty about the number. That rarely helps, and it often just adds shame. There is a better question.

Instead of asking "How long was I on?" ask "How do I feel now that I have stopped?" The time you spent matters far less than how the feed leaves you. Twenty minutes that leave you smiling at grandchildren's photos is time well spent. Five minutes that leave you anxious and cross is the feed taking more than it gave.

The move is

Each time you put the device down, pause and notice how you feel. Lighter or heavier? Calmer or more wound up? Connected or lonelier? Let that feeling, not the number of minutes, tell you whether the feed is serving you.

Confidence check

How you feel afterward is honest information. It is the clearest signal you have about whether a feed is good for you, and it is far kinder and more useful than counting minutes.

A gentle after-scroll check-in

This is not a test and there are no right answers. It is simply a moment of noticing. Try it the next few times you finish with a feed.

Your after-scroll check-in

1

Pause before you switch to something else. When you feel yourself ready to stop, take one slow breath before you set the device down.
2

Ask yourself one question: "Do I feel better or worse than when I started?" There is no wrong answer. You are only noticing.
3

Name the feeling in a single word. Calm, happy, tired, anxious, cross, connected, lonely. Just naming it puts you back in charge.
4

Decide what that tells you. If you usually feel worse, that is worth knowing. It does not mean quitting; it might mean scrolling a little less, or following different accounts. That is the next section.
Confidence check

You do not need to change anything today. Simply noticing how the feed leaves you, a few times, will start to shift how you use it, gently and on your own terms.

Small settings that hand you back control

You do not have to delete anything or give up the parts you enjoy. A few small changes make the feed calmer and easier to put down. Every one of these is reversible, and none of them cost anything.

Four calm settings to try

1

Turn off autoplay for videos. Autoplay starts the next video automatically, which pulls you along. In the app's Settings, look for "Media" or "Autoplay" and switch it off. Now videos play only when you choose to tap them.
2

Unfollow, mute, or hide what upsets you. On any post that leaves you angry, tap the three dots (•••) in the corner and choose "Hide," "Snooze," or "Unfollow." The person is not told, and your feed slowly becomes calmer.
3

Follow accounts that make you feel good. Seek out gardening groups, local history pages, your grandchildren's updates, and gentle hobby accounts. The more you tap and linger on these, the more of them the app will show you.
4

Set a soft time signal. Before you open a feed, set a simple kitchen timer or your tablet's timer for fifteen minutes. It is not a rule, just a friendly tap on the shoulder that says "you meant to do something else now."
A calmer way to catch the news

If it is the news that winds you up, consider reading it once a day from a trusted Canadian source, CBC, CTV, or your local paper, rather than letting alarming headlines find you all day long in a feed. You will still be well informed, and far less rattled.

Confidence check

None of these settings are permanent, and you can change them back any time. You are not breaking anything. You are simply shaping the feed to suit you, instead of the other way around.

Keeping what you love about it

This lesson is not asking you to give up social media. For many people it is the easiest way to see grandchildren's milestones, stay in touch with old friends, and feel less alone. The goal is simply to use it on your own terms.

  • Use it on purpose. Open the app to do a specific thing, see a photo, message a friend, check a group, then close it when that thing is done.
  • Let the feeling be your guide. If you finish feeling connected and happy, wonderful. If you finish feeling drained, that is a signal to change what you follow or how long you stay.
  • Remember you are the customer of your own time. The app is free to you because your attention is what it sells. Spending that attention where it makes you feel good is entirely your right.
Confidence check

Watching quietly, dipping in briefly, or stepping away for a while are all perfectly valid ways to use a feed. There is no correct amount. There is only what leaves you feeling well.

Quick answers

  • Is it bad for me to use Facebook or Instagram? Not at all. Used on your own terms, social media helps many seniors stay connected and feel less isolated. The trouble comes only when the feed pulls you along longer than you wanted and leaves you feeling worse. This lesson is about noticing that difference, not about quitting.
  • How much time on a feed is too much? There is no magic number, and counting minutes is not the most useful measure. A better guide is how you feel afterward. If you regularly finish feeling anxious, cross, or drained, that matters far more than whether it was ten minutes or forty.
  • Why does my feed show me so much upsetting news? Because posts that stir strong feelings get more reactions, and the app shows you more of whatever gets reactions. It is not a true picture of the world; it is whatever keeps people scrolling. You are always free to scroll past it or follow calmer accounts instead.
  • Will the app tell people if I unfollow or hide them? No. Unfollowing, muting, snoozing, or hiding a post is completely private. The other person is never notified. You can quietly shape your feed without any awkwardness.
