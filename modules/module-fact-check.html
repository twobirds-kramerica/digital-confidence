🔍 Knowing what is real

Is It True, or Does It Just Sound True?

⏱ About 15 minutes, at your own pace

✅ You are in a safe place. Nothing on this page can harm your device.

When you search for something online, or an AI answer tool writes you a reply, the answer comes back looking very sure of itself. Neat sentences. A confident tone. Here is the gentle truth: a confident tone is not the same as a correct answer. This lesson teaches one calm habit: three quiet questions to ask before you trust.

A familiar story

A reader told us about a small moment in her own family. Someone looked up a money question, read the very first result, and passed it along to everyone as settled fact. It turned out the first result was old, and the rule had since changed.

No harm was done in the end, but it stuck with her. The lesson was not "I am bad at this." The lesson was simply that the first answer is a starting point, not the finish line.

From your coach

I want you to hear this plainly: being fooled by a confident answer is not a personal failing. These tools are designed to sound certain, and they catch careful, intelligent people every day. The skill is not knowing everything. The skill is the small, calm pause before you trust. If you can slow down for one minute, you are already doing the hard part.

The three quiet questions

You do not need to become an expert or memorise anything complicated. Before you trust an answer that matters, you ask yourself three short questions.

  • 1. Who is telling me this? Is there a name, a company, or a website behind the answer, or is it just text floating on the screen?
  • 2. How old is it? Look for a date. Money rules, health advice, and prices change. An answer from years ago can be wrong now.
  • 3. Does a second place agree? Open one more website and see if it says the same thing. One source is a rumour. Two that agree is a start.
Confidence check

You are doing great, and your device is safe. Asking these questions cannot break anything. You are simply slowing down, and slowing down is exactly the skill.

Where to find the date and the source

When you read an answer, look near the top or the very bottom of the article for a small line of grey text. That line often holds the date it was written and the name of who wrote it. If you cannot find either one, that is your signal to be careful, not to trust it more.

Try it once (nothing is bought, nothing is changed)

This is safe. You will not spend money or change any setting. You are only practising the habit of checking.

A two-minute practice run

1

Think of a simple question you are curious about, like how much a first-class stamp costs in Canada right now.
2

Search for it, or ask your answer tool.
3

Read the first answer, then stop. Do not act on it.
4

Open one more website and look for the same answer. Notice whether they agree.
5

While you are there, find the date on one of them.
Success looks like this

You have finished when you have looked at two sources for the same question and found the date on at least one of them. The screen does not need to look like anything special. The win is that you checked before you trusted.

One gentle point about answer tools

AI answer tools want to be agreeable. If you tell one of them you believe something, it will often agree with you, even when you are mistaken, because agreeing keeps you happy. So treat its reply the way you would treat a chatty stranger's opinion: pleasant, possibly helpful, and worth checking before you rely on it.

If the question is about money or a scam

The 3-second rule

  1. ✋Stop If anything is asking you to send money or share a bank detail, do not act on the answer from a website or a tool.
  2. 🫁Breathe One slow, calm breath. Nothing genuine is lost by pausing.
  3. 🔍Verify Check with a real person or a trusted service first.
A Canadian service you can trust

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre: if you think something might be a scam, call toll-free 1-888-495-8501, or report online at antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca. It is run together by the RCMP, the Ontario Provincial Police, and the Competition Bureau. They estimate that fewer than 5 percent of fraud victims ever report, so when you call, you are helping protect other people too.

The move is

Before you trust or pass along an answer that matters, ask the three quiet questions: who is telling me this, how old is it, and does a second place agree? For anything about money, stop, breathe, and verify with a real person first.

Quick check: test your knowledge
