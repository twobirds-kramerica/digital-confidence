**Difficulty:** Beginner

[VOICEOVER]: "Welcome to Module 2 of the Digital Confidence Centre. This module is called The Security Shield, and it's one of the most important things you can learn. Understanding how scammers think and operate is genuinely protective — the more you know, the harder you are to fool. In this first video, we'll look at who runs these scams, what they're after, and how they target people."

---

### MAIN CONTENT

**SEGMENT 1: Who Are the Scammers?**
[VOICEOVER]: "Online scammers are not lone individuals sitting in a basement. Many are organised criminal operations — sometimes running out of call centres with dozens of employees, scripts, and sophisticated technology. They are professionals at what they do, and they specifically target older adults because research shows that older adults tend to be more trusting, more polite on the phone, and more likely to have retirement savings. This is not a reflection of anyone's intelligence. It's a reflection of how ruthless these operations are."
[VISUAL CUE]: Graphic showing a professional-looking call centre (no faces — just headsets and screens). Canadian flag and shield graphic.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Scammers are organised professionals — not your fault if they fool you

**SEGMENT 2: What They're After**
[VOICEOVER]: "Scammers want two things: money and information. Money means getting you to transfer funds, buy gift cards, or share your banking details. Information means getting your name, date of birth, Social Insurance Number, or passwords — things they can use to steal your identity and open accounts in your name. Both types are serious, but identity theft can take years to fully resolve. The important thing to know: once money is sent or information is given, it is very hard to recover. Prevention is everything."
[VISUAL CUE]: Two columns on screen: "They want MONEY" and "They want INFORMATION" with icons for each.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Two targets: your money and your personal information

**SEGMENT 3: How They Find You**
[VOICEOVER]: "Scammers find potential targets in several ways. Some just dial phone numbers randomly until someone answers. Others buy lists of names and addresses from data brokers — companies that collect and sell personal information. Some find people through social media if your profile is public. And some will use information from a data breach — when a company's records are hacked and customer information leaks out. The good news is you don't need to know how they found you. What matters is knowing what to do when they reach out."
[VISUAL CUE]: Simple graphic showing pathways: phone → scammer, social media → scammer, data breach → scammer.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: They find you through phone calls, social media, and data breaches

**SEGMENT 4: The Psychology of a Scam — Urgency and Fear**
[VOICEOVER]: "Every scam uses the same psychological tools: urgency and fear. 'Your account has been compromised.' 'You owe money to the CRA and police are on their way.' 'Your grandson is in trouble and needs money right now.' These messages are designed to make you panic — because when people panic, they stop thinking clearly and act fast. That's exactly what scammers count on. Here's the single most important fact in this entire module: a real organisation — the CRA, your bank, the police — will never demand immediate payment or threaten you with arrest over the phone."
[VISUAL CUE]: Scam script shown on screen with highlighted phrases: "URGENT," "ACT NOW," "or else." The highlighted phrases pulse to show how they create panic.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: URGENT and THREATENING = scam. Legitimate organisations do not work this way.

**SEGMENT 5: The Gift Card Tell**
[VOICEOVER]: "There is one sure-fire sign of a scam that almost never fails: if anyone — for any reason — tells you to pay for something using gift cards, it is a scam. Full stop. No legitimate government agency, utility company, bank, or business accepts gift cards as payment. If someone tells you to go to Shoppers Drug Mart, buy $500 in Google Play cards, and read them the numbers over the phone — hang up immediately. This is the most common payment method for scammers because once those numbers are given, the money is gone and untraceable."
[VISUAL CUE]: Gift card image with a large red X. Text: "NO real organisation accepts gift card payment."
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Asked to pay with gift cards? It is always a scam. Always.

---

### COMMON MISTAKES (1 minute)
[VOICEOVER]: "Two things that make people more vulnerable. First: staying on the line too long. Scammers are trained to keep you engaged — the longer they talk to you, the better they get at building trust. If something feels wrong, hang up. You are not being rude. You are being safe. Second: thinking 'it won't happen to me.' Scams target capable, intelligent people every day. Being alert protects you — assuming you're immune does not."
[VISUAL CUE]: A phone call scenario — someone staying on too long, looking uncertain. Then a confident hand pressing the hang-up button.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: It is never rude to hang up on a suspicious call

---

### RECAP (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "Three key takeaways. One: scammers are professionals targeting you on purpose — it's never your fault for being approached. Two: they use urgency and fear to make you act without thinking. Three: the gift card demand is a guaranteed scam signal. Take a breath, slow down, and if something feels wrong, it probably is."
[VISUAL CUE]: Three-point recap list on screen.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Urgency + fear = scam tactics · Gift cards = always a scam

## Video 2 of 3: Recognising Common Scam Tactics
**Target Duration:** 8 minutes
**Difficulty:** Beginner

### HOOK (First 15 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "What if I told you that scam messages almost always contain the same warning signs — and once you know what to look for, you'll be able to spot them in under 10 seconds? That's exactly what we're covering today."

### INTRODUCTION (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "In this video, we're going to walk through the most common types of scams that target Canadians: phone scams, email phishing, text message scams, and social media fraud. For each one, I'll show you exactly what it looks like and the red flags that give it away. By the end of this video, you'll have a mental checklist you can run through any time something suspicious arrives."
[VISUAL CUE]: Four icons: phone, email, text message, social media — each with a question mark. Title card.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Module 2 · Video 2 of 3: Recognising Scam Tactics

---

### MAIN CONTENT

**SEGMENT 1: Phone Scams — CRA and Police Impersonation**
[VOICEOVER]: "The most common phone scam in Canada impersonates the Canada Revenue Agency. The caller claims you owe back taxes and that police will arrest you unless you pay immediately. They'll often have a fake 'badge number' and sound very official. Here's what the real CRA never does: they never demand immediate payment by phone, they never threaten arrest, and they never ask for payment by gift cards, Bitcoin, or wire transfer. If you receive this call, hang up. Then, if you want to verify your actual tax situation, call the CRA directly at 1-800-959-8281 — a number you look up yourself, not one the caller gives you."
[VISUAL CUE]: Simulated phone screen showing an incoming call labelled "CRA." Script excerpt shown on screen with red flags highlighted.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Real CRA never threatens arrest or demands gift card payment

**SEGMENT 2: Email Phishing — Fake Bank Emails**
[VOICEOVER]: "Phishing — spelled P-H-I-S-H-I-N-G — is when scammers send fake emails pretending to be your bank, Canada Post, or a company like Netflix or Amazon. The email looks official and often says something like 'Your account has been suspended' or 'Unusual activity detected — click here to verify.' The goal is to get you to click a link and then enter your username and password on a fake website. Red flags to look for: the email address doesn't match the company (look for misspellings or random letters after the @), the greeting is generic ('Dear Customer,' not your name), and there's an urgent demand to act immediately."
[VISUAL CUE]: Fake bank email shown with four red flags annotated: sender email address, generic greeting, urgency language, suspicious link URL.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Check the sender's email address — one wrong letter = fake

**SEGMENT 3: Text Message Scams — Smishing**
[VOICEOVER]: "Scammers also send fraudulent text messages — sometimes called smishing. Common examples include: fake Canada Post delivery notices with a link to 'confirm your address,' fake prize notifications ('You've won a $500 Tim Hortons gift card!'), and fake account alerts from your bank. The same rules apply: don't click any link in a text you weren't expecting, even if it looks official. If you're curious whether it's real, go directly to the company's website by typing the address yourself, or call them using a number from their official site."
[VISUAL CUE]: Simulated text messages shown — one fake Canada Post notice, one fake prize text — with red flags annotated.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Never click links in unexpected texts — go to the website directly instead

**SEGMENT 4: Social Media Scams — Romance and Investment Fraud**
[VOICEOVER]: "Social media has opened new doors for scammers. Two of the most common are romance scams and investment scams. In a romance scam, someone builds a friendship or romantic connection with you over weeks or months — then eventually asks for money, usually for an 'emergency.' They're often extremely charming and attentive. In an investment scam, someone promises incredible returns on cryptocurrency or other investments, sometimes using a fake celebrity endorsement. Rule of thumb: anyone you've never met in person who eventually asks for money is almost certainly running a scam, no matter how well you feel you know them."
[VISUAL CUE]: Social media profile graphic (no real person's photo — an illustrated character) with a progression showing growing trust, then a money request with red flags.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Online friendship + money request = almost always a scam

**SEGMENT 5: The STOP Method — Your Personal Checklist**
[VOICEOVER]: "Here's a simple checklist I call the STOP Method for any message or call that makes you uncomfortable. S — Stop. Don't act immediately, no matter what they say. T — Think. Does this make sense? Would a real company contact me this way? O — Other source. Look up the organisation yourself and contact them directly using a number or website you find independently. P — Phone a friend. Before doing anything with money or passwords, call a trusted person and describe the situation. If it's a scam, a second set of eyes will usually catch it."
[VISUAL CUE]: STOP acronym builds on screen: S-T-O-P, each letter with its word and brief description.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: STOP · Think · Other source · Phone a friend

---

### COMMON MISTAKES (1 minute)
[VOICEOVER]: "Two mistakes that catch even cautious people. First: trusting caller ID. Scammers can fake the phone number that appears on your screen — a practice called spoofing. So even if your screen shows 'Royal Bank of Canada' or 'Government of Canada,' that does not guarantee it's real. If in doubt, hang up and call the number yourself from the official website. Second: feeling embarrassed to ask for help. Many people who recognise a scam midway through hesitate to hang up because they feel awkward. You owe no politeness to a scammer."
[VISUAL CUE]: Caller ID shown saying "Royal Bank" — then revealed to be a fake number. Confident hand hanging up.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Caller ID can be faked — hang up and call the real number yourself

---

### RECAP (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "Today's key points. One: CRA and police scams use fear of arrest — real agencies don't work that way. Two: phishing emails have red flags in the sender address, generic greetings, and urgent demands. Three: never click links in unexpected texts. Four: use the STOP Method — stop, think, find another source, phone a friend. You now have a real toolkit."
[VISUAL CUE]: Four-point summary on screen.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: STOP Method: Stop · Think · Other source · Phone a friend

---

[VOICEOVER]: "In our final video for this module, we'll cover what to do if you think you've already been targeted — who to call, how to protect yourself immediately, and how to report it. It's an important one. See you in Video 3."

## Video 3 of 3: What To Do If You've Been Targeted
**Target Duration:** 7 minutes
**Difficulty:** Beginner

### HOOK (First 15 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "If you've ever worried that you might have already given information to a scammer — or if you think it might have happened to someone you love — this video is for you. There are clear steps you can take right now, and taking action quickly makes a real difference."

### INTRODUCTION (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "Being targeted by a scam is not a sign of weakness or foolishness — it happens to sharp, cautious people every day. What matters is what you do next. In this video, we'll walk through exactly what steps to take if you think you've been scammed: who to call, how to protect your accounts, and how to report it so others don't go through the same thing."
[VISUAL CUE]: Title card. Calm, reassuring colour palette — deep blue, not alarming red.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Module 2 · Video 3 of 3: What To Do If You've Been Targeted

---

### MAIN CONTENT

**SEGMENT 1: Don't Panic — Take a Breath First**
[VOICEOVER]: "The very first step is the hardest one: stop, take a breath, and do not send any more money or give any more information while you're still feeling panicked or uncertain. Scammers often call back immediately after a first contact to keep the pressure up — 'Did you get the gift cards? We need those numbers right now.' Do not answer further calls from unknown numbers until you've had a chance to speak with someone you trust."
[VISUAL CUE]: Phone showing incoming call from "Unknown Number" — person choosing not to answer. Calm, deliberate action.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Step 1: Stop sending money or information. Let unknown calls go to voicemail.

**SEGMENT 2: Call Your Bank Immediately**
[VOICEOVER]: "If you've given out any banking information — account numbers, passwords, card details — your first call should be to your bank. Call the number on the back of your debit or credit card, or go to your bank's official website to find the fraud line. Tell them what happened, even if you're embarrassed. Banks deal with this every day and their fraud teams are there to help you, not to judge you. They can freeze suspicious transactions, cancel compromised cards, and in some cases reverse recent transfers."
[VISUAL CUE]: Back of a bank card with the customer service number highlighted. Then a phone call being made.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Bank fraud lines are free, confidential, and available 24/7

**SEGMENT 3: Report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre**
[VOICEOVER]: "Canada has a national fraud reporting organisation called the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. Their phone number is 1-888-495-8501 and their website is antifraudcentre.ca. Reporting your experience — even if you didn't lose any money — helps them track patterns, shut down scam operations, and warn other Canadians. You don't need to have lost money to make a report. Even reporting a call or email you didn't fall for is valuable."
[VISUAL CUE]: Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre website shown. Phone number displayed large and clearly.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre: 1-888-495-8501 | antifraudcentre.ca

**SEGMENT 4: Change Your Passwords**
[VOICEOVER]: "If a scammer may have seen or obtained any of your passwords — or if you gave them access to your device — change your passwords as soon as possible. Start with the most important ones: your email password and your banking app password. If you're not sure how to change a password, Module 3 of this course covers passwords in detail. For now, the priority is to change them quickly before they can be used."
[VISUAL CUE]: iPad screen showing Settings > Password change screen. Step-by-step but fast (just the concept, not a full tutorial).
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Priority: change your email and banking passwords first

**SEGMENT 5: Talk to Someone You Trust**
[VOICEOVER]: "One of the most important steps that's also the most often skipped: tell someone. A family member, a trusted friend, your doctor if the stress is affecting you. Fraud is deeply upsetting and many people carry the shame of it alone. You should not. The shame belongs to the people who did this — not to you. Talking about it also helps prevent it from happening again, because the person you tell can help watch for follow-up contacts from the same scammers."
[VISUAL CUE]: Two people sitting together, one comforting the other. Simple illustrated style, no real photos.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: You are not alone. Talking about it helps — and the shame is not yours.

---

### COMMON MISTAKES (1 minute)
[VOICEOVER]: "Two responses that make the situation worse. First: sending more money to try to 'get your money back.' This is a common follow-up scam — the same criminals call back claiming to be recovery agents who can retrieve your lost funds, for a fee. There are no legitimate recovery services that operate this way. Second: waiting to report it. The sooner you contact your bank and the Anti-Fraud Centre, the better the chance of limiting the damage. Many people wait days out of embarrassment — but early reporting genuinely matters."
[VISUAL CUE]: A second fraudulent call — "recovery agent" — with a clear red X. Timeline graphic showing: early report = better outcome.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: "Recovery agent" offers are always a second scam — do not pay them

---

### RECAP (30 seconds)
[VOICEOVER]: "If you think you've been targeted: one, stop all contact and don't send anything more. Two, call your bank's fraud line immediately. Three, report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at 1-888-495-8501. Four, change your most important passwords. Five, talk to someone you trust. You are not alone in this, and taking action protects you and others."
[VISUAL CUE]: Five-step list builds on screen.
[ON-SCREEN TEXT]: Act quickly · Call your bank · Report to 1-888-495-8501

---
