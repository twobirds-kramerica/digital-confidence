Who's Paying for Your Attention?

Free apps. Countdown timers. Sponsored creators. The 45-minute scroll you did not plan. Four scenarios on how attention gets bought and sold online.

✅ This is a safe space to explore.
Nothing on this page can harm your device, there is no grade, and you cannot break anything. Question everything — that is the whole point.
❤️ From your coach

If you have ever opened an app for one second and lost 45 minutes — that was not a personal failure. It was an engineering decision made by a company that earns money every second you stay. Understanding how that works does not mean you have to quit social media. It means you get to decide how much of your attention is worth giving, and to whom.

Scenario 1 of 4
1
Digital Business Model
The App That's Free
TikTok costs nothing to download. Instagram costs nothing. YouTube costs nothing. Yet the companies behind them are worth hundreds of billions of dollars — TikTok's parent company ByteDance is valued at over $300 billion CAD. Nobody is paying. Where is the money coming from?

How do apps like TikTok make money if users never pay?

You are the product

Every second you scroll is revenue. Advertisers pay TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube to put their message in front of you — specifically you, based on your age, location, watch history, and hundreds of other signals the platform has collected. The app is free because you are not the customer. You are what's being sold. A business that earns money every second you stay has one engineering goal: keep you watching as long as possible.

2
Dark Patterns
The Ticket That Expires in 10 Minutes
You find concert tickets online. The page shows: "⏰ Only 2 seats left at this price! Your cart expires in 09:47." A timer counts down. You feel a pull to buy now before you lose them. You haven't checked if the price is reasonable or if other sites have the same seats.

What is a countdown timer on a shopping site most likely doing?

False urgency is a feature, not a bug

Dark patterns are design choices built into websites and apps that work against the user on purpose. Countdown timers, "only 3 left" warnings, and hidden unsubscribe buttons are all in the same family. Studies show that a 10-minute countdown increases purchase rates by 30% or more — even when the timer resets the moment it hits zero. The rule: if you feel rushed to click, that is the signal to slow down. Real scarcity does not need a blinking clock. Open a second tab, compare prices, and decide on your own schedule.

3
Influencer Marketing
The Creator Who "Swears By" This Supplement
A YouTuber with 1.2 million subscribers posts a video. "I've been trying this supplement for three months and I genuinely swear by it. My energy is completely different." Buried three lines into the description: "This video is sponsored by NutriMax." They seem sincere. They've mentioned personal health struggles in past videos. The supplement costs $89 CAD.

How should you read this recommendation compared to one from a friend?

Paid endorsements change the incentive

In Canada, paid endorsements must be disclosed under the Competition Act — which is why "Sponsored" appears in the description. The disclosure doesn't mean the creator is lying. They may genuinely use the product. But a creator who earns $10,000 for a sponsored video has a financial reason to be enthusiastic, separate from their actual experience. That is not dishonesty — it is a changed incentive. Apply the same healthy skepticism you would to any advertisement, regardless of how sincere the person seems. Before a $89 purchase: look for independent reviews, check Health Canada's natural health product database, and ask whether you would have wanted this product before you saw the video.

4
Attention Engineering
The 45-Minute Scroll You Didn't Plan
You open Instagram to check one notification. Forty-five minutes later you are deep in content you never set out to find — videos, stories, reels, suggested posts from accounts you've never seen. You did not plan to stay. You do not feel particularly refreshed. You are not sure how it happened.

Why is it so difficult to stop scrolling on social media?

The missing stop sign

The infinite scroll patent was filed explicitly to remove the natural pause of page turns — the moment where you would have decided whether to keep going. Variable reward — the mechanism also found in slot machines — keeps you scrolling because the next piece of content might be interesting, funny, or surprising. Both of these are deliberate engineering decisions, not side effects. The average Canadian teenager spends 4+ hours per day on social media. That is not 4 hours of free choice — most of it is engineered drift. Knowing this does not mean you have to quit. It means you can set your own stopping points instead of waiting for one that was never built.
