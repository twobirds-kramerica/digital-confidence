## [POST 2] [WEEK 1] [DCC LAUNCH STORY] [CTA: Link to Digital Confidence Centre]

I built a 241-page digital literacy platform for Ontario seniors in March 2026. Here's what I learned about trust-first design.

My mum is 72. She's smart, capable, and deeply suspicious of anything on her iPad that she didn't ask for. When I started building the Digital Confidence Centre, I realised I wasn't building a course. I was building a trust relationship with someone who has every reason to be cautious.

Trust-first design means the close button is always visible. It means every lesson starts by telling you what won't happen — no charges, no downloads, no tricks. It means the font is large, the language is plain, and every single screen was tested with real seniors before it went live.

We built 11 modules covering everything from passwords to banking to understanding AI. Every module starts with "you're safe here" and ends with "you did it."

The response has been quiet but real. Families are sending it to parents. A retirement home in St. Thomas asked about running it as a group programme. Connected Canadians, who provide free tech support for seniors across Ontario, have started recommending it.

I didn't build this to scale. I built it because my mum deserved it, and so does yours.

## [POST 6] [WEEK 3] [TRUST-FIRST DESIGN] [CTA: Soft link to DCC as example]

Every product I've ever built has had one thing in common: if people don't trust it, they won't use it.

This is the thing most AI consultants get wrong. They lead with capability. "Look what this tool can do." But capability without trust is just a demo that nobody adopts.

When I worked at Telus, I watched million-dollar platforms fail because the people who were supposed to use them didn't believe the system had their back. The technology was fine. The trust wasn't there.

Now I build everything trust-first. With seniors, that means big close buttons and plain language. With SMEs, it means showing the ROI on one small workflow before asking anyone to change their whole operation. With AI adoption, it means being honest about what the tool can't do before you show what it can.

Trust-first design isn't a methodology. It's a decision to put the user's anxiety ahead of your feature list. Every time.

If you want to see what trust-first looks like in practice, the Digital Confidence Centre is a good example:
https://twobirds-kramerica.github.io/digital-confidence/

What went right: Claude Code is genuinely extraordinary at turning a clear brief into working code. If you know exactly what you want, you can move at a speed that didn't exist two years ago. I wrote the product specs. Claude wrote the code. I reviewed, edited, and deployed. The loop was tight.

What was hard: AI doesn't know your users. Every piece of generated code needed human judgment — does this make sense for a 75-year-old on an iPad? Is this wording going to scare someone? Does this button actually go where it should? The craft is still human. The velocity is AI.

What it taught me: the future of product work isn't AI replacing people. It's one experienced person with AI doing what used to take a team of ten. That's the real disruption — and it's already here.
