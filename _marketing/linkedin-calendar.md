## WEEK 5 — Category: A (DCC Feature/Tip)
**Post Day:** Tuesday
**Content Type:** Image suggestion — side-by-side: real CRA letter vs scam phone call

One of the most effective things we've ever done for senior digital safety:

Teach people the exact words scammers use.

The CRA scam has been running for years — and it still works because it uses three specific pressure tactics:

1. **Threat of arrest** — "Police are on their way"
2. **Urgency** — "You must pay today or face consequences"
3. **Unusual payment** — "Buy iTunes gift cards and read us the numbers"

Once someone knows these three patterns, the scam falls apart immediately. The fear doesn't take hold because they recognise what's happening.

Module 2 at Digital Confidence Centre walks through this in detail. It's written for seniors, in plain Canadian English, with specific examples of what a real CRA contact looks like vs a scam.

The real CRA sends letters. They give you time. They never ask for gift cards.

Share this with someone you think needs to hear it.

How do you know if an app is real?

This question comes up constantly with older adults — and the answer is simpler than most people think.

The four-second check:
1. Who is the developer? (Look below the app name)
2. How many reviews does it have?
3. Is the description professionally written?
4. Does it ask for permissions that make sense?

A real TD Bank app is made by "TD Bank." A real weather app from The Weather Network is made by "Pelmorex Media." If the developer name looks generic or unfamiliar — pause.

Module 4 at Digital Confidence Centre covers App Store safety in detail, including how to find a bank's real app, what to do with suspicious permissions, and how to safely delete an app if you've had doubts.

## WEEK 17 — Category: A (DCC Feature/Tip)
**Post Day:** Tuesday
**Content Type:** Image suggestion — email inbox with sender address highlighted

Every phishing email has a tell.

It's usually in the sender address — not the display name, which can say anything, but the actual email address. You can find it on an iPad by tapping the sender's displayed name.

A real email from TD Bank will come from @td.com.
A real email from the CRA will come from @canada.ca.
A phishing email will come from something like service@td-bank-secure.net — extra words, hyphens, unusual domains.

That one check — sender address — eliminates the majority of phishing attempts immediately.

Module 5 at Digital Confidence Centre covers email safety in detail: reading emails, spotting phishing, and how to unsubscribe safely without accidentally confirming your address to a scammer.

## WEEK 25 — Category: A (DCC Feature/Tip)
**Post Day:** Tuesday
**Content Type:** Text only

The 3-Second Rule.

It sounds almost too simple. But we've seen it work, again and again, in real situations.

When something unexpected appears on your screen — a pop-up, a warning, a message you didn't expect — your first instinct is to react. Tap it away. Call the number. Click the link. The urgency feels real.

The 3-Second Rule interrupts that instinct.

Stop. Breathe. Count three seconds.

In those three seconds, your thinking brain has a chance to catch up to your startled reaction. You start asking: did I expect this? Does this make sense? Can I verify this another way?

Three seconds is enough to save someone from clicking a phishing link. Three seconds is enough to prevent a CRA scam payment.

We put this rule in Module 2 at Digital Confidence Centre. It's also in every video script we've written. It's the one habit that does the most work.

Share this post with someone who could use it.

## WEEK 29 — Category: A (DCC Feature/Tip)
**Post Day:** Tuesday
**Content Type:** Image suggestion — password screen with a passphrase example

Four words. That's all it takes.

The strongest passwords aren't the ones with random symbols and numbers that nobody can remember. They're four unrelated ordinary words strung together.

"purple maple kettle rain"

That's 22 characters. It would take a computer millions of years to guess it. And a 73-year-old can remember it by picturing a purple maple tree in the rain next to a kettle.

This is called a passphrase — and it's the method security experts actually recommend. Not "P@ssw0rd1!" — that's guessable because it follows a predictable pattern.

Module 3 at Digital Confidence Centre covers passwords and passphrases in detail, alongside iCloud Keychain (which saves all your passwords so you don't have to remember them) and Face ID setup.

**Content Type:** Text only

Here's a finding from senior digital literacy research that I keep returning to:

The biggest barrier to digital adoption among older adults is not cost, and it's not access.

It's fear of making a mistake.

Specifically: fear of doing something irreversible. Fear of accidentally sending the wrong thing, deleting something important, or "breaking" the device.

This fear is irrational — tablets are very hard to permanently damage through normal use — but it is completely understandable if nobody has told you that.

The fix is embarrassingly simple: tell people, early and clearly, what cannot happen. Tell them the Home button is safe. Tell them the worst case for most mistakes is "you close the app and reopen it."

Reassurance, delivered credibly, removes the blocker entirely.

That's why every module at DCC starts with what you cannot accidentally break. Not features. Not functions. Safety.

A question we hear all the time:

"What do I do if I've already clicked something suspicious?"

The answer, in order:

1. **Don't panic.** Visiting a suspicious website on an iPad is very unlikely to cause permanent damage. Apple's security architecture is strong.

2. **Press Home.** Get out of the browser or app immediately.

3. **Restart your iPad.** Simple, effective, takes two minutes.

4. **If financial information was involved, call your bank.** Use the number on the back of your card — not any number from the suspicious message.

5. **If you're in Ontario, report it** to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at 1-888-495-8501.

Module 2 at Digital Confidence Centre covers this full response in detail — and importantly, it starts with reassurance rather than alarm.
